# Limits of Independent Security Verification on Vendor-Locked Devices

## Abstract

Vendor-locked devices, most prominently stock Android phones with a locked bootloader and no administrator access, are general-purpose computers whose owners are nonetheless structurally prevented from fully inspecting, controlling, or verifying the software running on their own device. Taking Android as its worked example, this paper systematizes that design for an owner who treats security as the ability to audit the device rather than the platform's ability to enforce policy. It introduces owner-inspectability -- the property that an owner can examine and verify the whole system from a trusted vantage point without forfeiting the attested status the device reports to software -- and organizes the resulting limits: the owner cannot inspect the whole system from a clean environment, nor reach storage and memory without withheld privileges; application and update delivery is vendor-controlled, so compromise or coercion could push targeted malicious installs; and remote attestation ties the status a remote party relies on to the device staying unmodified, so enabling inspection forfeits the very status it validates. Obtaining root does not dissolve these limits: root is bounded by the platform's own defenses and can be deceived by a kernel-space rootkit that falsifies what in-system tools observe. Many applications also refuse to run without a hardware-backed integrity verdict an owner-controlled configuration cannot produce, so the owner is squeezed from both sides -- declining to modify forecloses inspection, while modifying is costly and forfeits the status everyday use depends on. Against a conventional computer the owner fully controls, most established malware-analysis techniques are unavailable on a stock phone without first breaking its own verified-boot and attestation guarantees; surveying the main objections, the paper concludes that limited observability leaves absence of findings as weak evidence of integrity. It covers stock Android and extends to other vendor-locked devices only to varying degrees.

## 1. Introduction

On a vendor-locked device the owner is, in practice, obliged to trust the platform operator and device vendor -- on a stock Android phone, Google and the manufacturer -- for both application delivery and system updates, because the device cannot be fully examined from the outside while it remains locked down. If that trust relationship is abused -- through compromise of the delivery infrastructure, coercion of an operator, or a malicious insider -- targeted malicious installations or updates may be difficult to detect, independently verify, or prove to a third party.

The same constraints bear on ordinary malware investigation. When a device is suspected of infection, the owner typically cannot perform a thorough inspection from a separate, known-clean computer without first unlocking or modifying the device. That modification can alter the evidence under examination, and a well-constructed backdoor could detect the onset of an investigation and erase itself or conceal its traces.

This paper treats these properties not as implementation accidents but as consequences of a design in which administrative capability is deliberately withheld from the owner. It catalogs the resulting verification limits, grounds its claims in the available technical and forensic literature where such sources exist, and distinguishes claims supported by cited evidence from claims that remain asserted without a specific source.

This is a systematization-and-position paper: it introduces no new experiment or dataset and instead organizes existing technical and forensic evidence around a single owner-centric criterion. Its contributions are:

- it names and defines owner-inspectability as the property an owner needs in order to audit a device, and distinguishes it from the related but distinct notion of organizational forensic readiness;
- it catalogs, against that criterion, the specific verification limits of stock Android and traces each to the same deliberate design choice rather than to isolated implementation defects;
- it shows that root access -- as distinct from the full administrative control withheld from the owner -- does not restore owner-inspectability on a modern hardened device, because root is itself bounded by mandatory access control, kernel lockdown, module-signature enforcement, and a hardware-rooted verified-boot chain, and because root can in any case be deceived by a kernel-space rootkit that falsifies what any in-system tool reports;
- it documents how remote attestation, and the applications that depend on it, increasingly penalize owner-controlled configurations, deepening the circular trust problem; and
- it provides a structured capability comparison with a fully controlled host that makes the aggregate effect legible as a single design trade-off.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 summarizes the platform mechanisms. Section 3 states the threat model, scope, and terminology, including the constrained meaning of root and the definition of owner-inspectability. Section 4 situates the work in prior literature. Sections 5 through 11 catalog the verification limits. Section 12 addresses objections, including aftermarket operating systems. Section 13 discusses the findings and their limitations, and Section 14 concludes.

## 2. Background

Android's security model is built around several mutually reinforcing mechanisms. Android Verified Boot (AVB) establishes a chain of trust from an immutable hardware root of trust through the bootloader and operating-system partitions, so that only software signed by the vendor executes during startup; a locked bootloader refuses to load or boot any image that is not so signed. Application sandboxing isolates each application, giving it a distinct kernel-level user identity and a private data directory that other applications cannot read. Mandatory Access Control, implemented through SELinux, confines even privileged system processes to narrowly defined domains, so that a compromise of one component does not automatically yield access to the whole system. File-based encryption and full-disk encryption protect data at rest, with the principal encryption key bound to dedicated hardware.

These mechanisms are effective at their stated purpose: constraining what ordinary software, including most malicious software, can do once it is running on the device. That effectiveness is real but neither symmetric nor complete. A sufficiently capable adversary is not reliably excluded by it -- as Sections 8 and 12 develop, such an adversary can compromise the already-booted, legitimately signed system at runtime, or arrive inside the vendor-signed image itself, without the device failing attestation, and verified boot has demonstrated defeats beneath the operating system. The same design, meanwhile, withholds from the device owner the administrative privileges that would permit raw disk access, live memory capture, kernel instrumentation, or the booting of an external trusted environment. The owner is therefore placed not in the same position as an attacker but in a worse one: the protections that defend the platform against modification exclude the owner -- and the security researcher who would audit the device on the owner's behalf -- more reliably than they exclude the capable adversary the owner has most reason to fear. It is natural to read these mechanisms as existing chiefly for the owner's protection; that is only half the picture. They do keep unauthorized third-party software off the device, but the same signed-image and attestation machinery also enforces objectives the owner may neither share nor be able to remove or audit -- that only a vendor-approved configuration runs, with application and service access gated by relying parties rather than by the owner -- so the guarantee runs in part to the platform and those relying parties, not to the owner alone. This paper treats those platform-control and content-protection objectives as out of scope to analyze (Section 3) and returns, for attestation, to whom that guarantee actually runs (Section 8).

## 3. Threat Model and Scope

This paper defines security from the perspective of the end user and device owner: the ability to control, inspect, and independently verify the device. Under this definition a device is more secure when its owner can establish, from a trusted vantage point, whether it is running only the software they intend and what that software is doing -- free of malware or backdoors, whether planted by an outside attacker or by the platform itself -- and can produce evidence of that state that a skeptical third party could check.

Three practical capabilities are treated as the goals of interest: inspecting the device deeply for malware and backdoors; proving the result to a skeptical third party; and creating complete device backups suitable for independent verification.

Explicitly out of scope are digital-rights-management objectives and platform-control goals -- that is, the platform's interest in enforcing policy against the owner, in guaranteeing content protection, or in ensuring that only vendor-approved configurations run. These objectives frequently stand in tension with owner-centric verifiability, and conflating the two obscures the specific limitations examined here. Where the platform regards a property as a security feature and the owner regards the same property as an obstacle to verification, this paper describes it from the owner's standpoint and notes the platform's view where relevant.

Although this paper documents Android in detail as its worked example -- it is the most openly specified vendor-locked platform -- the owner-inspectability criterion and the limits it exposes are not Android-specific. They follow from vendor-lock itself: a locked boot chain, administrative capability withheld from the owner, and attestation that ties the device's standing with apps and services to a vendor-approved state. By the same logic they apply, to varying degrees, to other vendor-locked devices, Apple iOS among them; the mechanisms differ in detail, but the owner's structural position does not. This paper references such platforms only where a cited source speaks to them and does not document them to the same depth.

Terminology is used consistently throughout. Stock Android denotes a typical device as sold, with a locked bootloader and without root access.

Root access denotes uid-0 authority within the running operating system: the ability, in principle, to read and write files, inspect processes, and alter system components that an ordinary user cannot. The term is inherited from Unix, where "root" is the superuser account; Android, however, exposes no such login account. uid 0 exists as a kernel identity held by init and a deliberately shrinking set of system daemons, but a stock production ("user") build ships no owner-accessible "su" and refuses to run its debug bridge (adbd) as root, so the owner cannot assume that identity at all without first modifying the device.

Equally important is what this authority does not include on a modern hardened device, because the common assumption that root confers total control of the machine no longer holds. Root runs inside, and is bounded by, the platform's other defenses. Mandatory access control through SELinux confines even uid-0 processes to permitted domains, so that superuser identity does not by itself grant unrestricted operations [@aosp-selinux]. The kernel's lockdown facility, once engaged, withdraws a range of otherwise root-accessible interfaces that could be used to modify the running kernel [@kernel-lockdown]. Module-signature enforcement can require that any code loaded into the kernel carry a valid signature, so that root cannot insert an arbitrary kernel module [@kernel-module-signing], and the kernel.modules_disabled control, once set, blocks all further module loading until reboot in a one-way fashion that even root cannot reverse [@kernel-modules-disabled]. Beneath the operating system, Android Verified Boot and a locked bootloader anchor the boot chain to a hardware root of trust and refuse unsigned images, so that root -- an in-system privilege -- cannot by itself rewrite the firmware or boot chain that sit below it [@aosp-verified-boot], [@aosp-bootloader-locking]. Throughout this paper, therefore, "root" names a powerful but bounded in-system privilege, not unconstrained control of the kernel, firmware, or boot chain; Section 10 and the discussion in Section 13 return to why obtaining it still does not deliver owner-inspectability.

One honesty caveat attaches to the mechanisms just named: Android's Generic Kernel Image does not enforce a valid signature on every loadable kernel module -- module signing is not supported for GKI vendor modules, and on devices that require verified boot the authenticity of kernel modules rests instead on dm-verity over the read-only partitions that hold them [@aosp-loadable-kernel-modules] rather than on per-module signature enforcement. The mechanisms above are capabilities of the platform, deployed to differing degrees across devices, not uniform guarantees on every device.

Administrative control, by contrast, denotes the broader authority an owner traditionally holds over a general-purpose computer: the ability to replace the bootloader, kernel, kernel modules, initramfs, and operating system, and to boot an external trusted environment of the owner's choosing, with uid-0 inside a system so constituted. On a traditional personal computer this authority over the operating-system and boot stack is the owner's by default; on stock Android it is withheld from the owner and conferred only on the vendor. Root, when an owner obtains it at all, is a fragment of this authority -- superuser standing inside the vendor's already-booted operating system, without command of the kernel and boot chain beneath it -- which is why, as Sections 8, 10, and 13 develop, obtaining root does not amount to administrative control and does not by itself deliver owner-inspectability. Section 13 contrasts the two stacks directly, including the firmware layers that remain opaque to the owner on both.

A clean, trusted environment denotes a separate computer or externally booted system under the examiner's control, as opposed to the device's own currently running operating system.

Owner-inspectability is the central property this paper isolates. A device is owner-inspectable when its owner can examine and verify the whole system -- storage, memory, and the boot and operating-system state -- from a clean, trusted vantage point, and can do so without forfeiting the attested status the device reports to the software and services it runs -- the vendor-approved standing a remote relying party, not the owner, relies on. The term is deliberately distinct from forensic readiness as defined in the digital-forensics literature, which concerns an organization's advance preparation to collect and preserve admissible evidence about its own systems and users [@rowlingson-forensic-readiness], [@iso-27043]: forensic readiness is a posture an institution arranges for investigators, whereas owner-inspectability is a property the device either affords its owner or withholds. Owner-inspectability is also narrower than a general right to modify or repair a device: it requires that the owner be able to establish, and to demonstrate, what the device is running -- not that the device be freely modifiable. That separation is clean only for persistent, at-rest state: inspecting the files, installed packages, and boot chain a device holds at rest is a read-only act that requires no modification. Detecting non-persistent, memory-resident malware is different, because such code leaves no durable artifact and can be observed only through live memory acquisition or runtime and kernel instrumentation [@sudhakar-fileless-malware], which on a locked device is itself a modification -- unlocking, rooting, or booting a custom kernel [@yang-live-memory-acquisition] -- that, as Section 8 shows, forfeits the device's attested status. For such live threats inspectability and modifiability cannot be cleanly separated, and the squeeze on the owner deepens rather than eases. AOSP in principle allows a device to be locked to a user-controlled root of trust rather than only to the vendor's [@aosp-verified-boot]; whether that latent capability is usable in practice, and whether it survives contact with the attestation ecosystem, is examined in Sections 8 and 12.

## 4. Related Work

The platform mechanisms examined here are documented authoritatively in the Android platform security model, which sets out verified boot, application sandboxing, mandatory access control, and attestation as a coherent design and states the threat model they address [@mayrhofer-android-security-model]. That account is written from the platform's standpoint -- the device defending itself, its users' data, and the ecosystem against a running adversary. The present paper adopts the complementary standpoint of the owner who wishes to audit the device, and reads the same mechanisms for what they deny that owner.

Three bodies of prior work supply the evidence reorganized here. The mobile-forensics literature establishes how, and how far, data can be acquired from Android devices: official guidance on sound acquisition and its preconditions [@nist-sp800-101r1], practitioner treatments of imaging and temporary-root techniques [@hoog-android-forensics], and field reports on the limited traces recoverable from stock devices in spyware investigations [@amnesty-pegasus-methodology], [@mvt-android-methodology]. The rootkit-detection literature establishes that trustworthy detection of kernel-level compromise requires a vantage point outside the running operating system, and explores hardware-assisted acquisition to obtain one [@guri-joker-jtag], [@bickford-rootkits-smartphones], [@brodbeck-covert-rootkit-detection]. The anti-forensics and evasion literature establishes that malicious code routinely detects analysis environments and removes its own traces [@chen-anti-virtualization], [@ruggia-evasive-malware], [@garfinkel-anti-forensics].  Each cluster is examined in detail in the sections that follow; the contribution of this paper is not new measurement within any one of them but their systematization around owner-inspectability.

The owner-inspectability criterion relates to, but is distinct from, organizational forensic readiness [@rowlingson-forensic-readiness], [@iso-27043] and from remote-attestation design [@mayrhofer-android-security-model], [@play-integrity-improvements]; the distinctions are drawn where each is used. To the author's knowledge, the limits of owner-side verification on stock Android have not previously been assembled into a single, criterion-driven account.

## 5. Independent Verification Limits

On most stock devices it is difficult to independently verify the state of the whole system. When malware, a hidden backdoor, or tampering is suspected, the owner generally cannot examine the system from a clean, trusted environment without first modifying the device. The owner is therefore left to trust the vendor-controlled software and services that deliver applications and updates, even though those channels could themselves be abused in a targeted manner.

The underlying cause is architectural. Android is commonly shipped as a locked-down general-purpose computer in which administrative capability is deliberately withheld from the owner and the bootloader is locked. Many protections and integrity checks are implemented under vendor control, limiting what the owner can change, inspect, or replace. As a result the owner is typically prevented from inspecting the whole system and from producing a complete device backup without first bypassing these restrictions.

These restrictions bear on the three goals defined above. First, deep inspection for malware and backdoors is impeded: on a non-rooted device, the private files of other applications are inaccessible, and system areas and logs are access-controlled rather than openly readable [@mayrhofer-android-security-model], [@aosp-selinux], which makes it harder to confirm whether suspicious behavior originates from malware or from a normal system component, and harder to rule out targeted tampering. Second, the owner cannot readily prove a clean or compromised result to a third party, because the evidence that would support such a proof cannot be collected. Third, a complete device backup -- for example, a full offline image of the relevant partitions -- generally requires privileges the device restricts; on a non-rooted device "full backup" in practice means a partial backup mediated by the operating system and by individual applications [@android-allowbackup], [@aosp-auto-backup].

For the same reasons, demonstrating the presence or absence of a hidden backdoor is difficult. An examiner who cannot fully inspect system files, logs, and low-level components without first modifying the device is forced to rely on what the running operating system chooses to reveal -- precisely the situation that a well-concealed backdoor would seek to establish.

## 6. Vulnerability to Targeted Malicious Upgrades

Because key parts of application and update delivery are controlled by the platform operator and device vendor -- on Android, Google Play for applications and the manufacturer for system firmware -- vendor-locked devices are exposed to the risk of targeted malicious application installations, application upgrades, and system upgrades.

A documented and ordinary feature illustrates the mechanism. Many devices permit a user to sign in to the Google Play web or desktop store using the same Google account, typically the same email address, that is used on the device. Selecting "Install" for an application in the web interface prompts the user, where multiple devices are registered, to choose a target device; the application is then installed on that device. This demonstrates that Google Play can initiate an installation to a specific, linked device without direct physical interaction with that device [@google-play-help].

The concern is not that an arbitrary outsider can invoke this capability without authorization. The concern is that control of the delivery system is itself powerful. If Google Play's infrastructure were compromised, or if a privileged insider were malicious or coerced, this capability could in principle be abused to push a backdoored version of an application to a specific target. By the same logic, device manufacturers control firmware and system updates, so targeted malicious system upgrades are likewise possible if that control is abused.

Update-related communication is, moreover, not anonymous on devices certified for Google Mobile Services (GMS). Google Play services periodically checks in with Google, transmitting persistent hardware identifiers -- the IMEI, hardware serial number, network MAC address, and Google Services Framework identifier -- that Google documents as associated with the signed-in Google account and used, among other purposes, to determine a device's eligibility for software updates and security patches [@leith-handset-privacy], [@google-device-config-service]. Whether a particular firmware update is delivered through Google or through the device vendor varies by manufacturer. The AOSP security policy itself grants Google Play services access to the system-update service and to the A/B update engine, confirming its privileged role in the update path on such devices [@aosp-sepolicy-gmscore].

Network-level anonymization does not neutralize this exposure, because the exposure is at the identity layer rather than the network layer. Using Google Play on a stock Android device requires the device to be signed in to a Google account; the store cannot be browsed or used to install applications without one [@google-play-help]. Every interaction with Google Play authenticates that account to Google, so each install or update request is bound to a specific, persistent identity. Routing the device's traffic through Tor conceals the network (IP) address from which a request originates, and in that narrow sense remains functional; but the account-based authentication required by the operating system still presents the user's Google identity to Google on every request. The network layer is thus anonymized while the identity layer is not: Tor can hide where a connection comes from, yet the device itself discloses who is making it.

## 7. Limitations of Internal Storage Access on Non-Rooted Devices

Accessing internal storage for purposes such as data recovery or malware and rootkit analysis is not reasonably easy on most stock devices. The guiding principle of malware analysis and backdoor hunting is that a device suspected of compromise should not be relied upon to examine itself [@rfc3227]; the data should ideally be inspected from a separate, known-clean computer, in keeping with established forensic guidance on sound acquisition and evidence preservation [@nist-sp800-101r1]. Stock Android obstructs exactly this.

The obstruction has two roots. First, internal storage is typically a soldered component that cannot be removed without specialized work [@nist-sp800-101r1]. Second, Android normally blocks access to many system files and to the private files of other applications unless the device is rooted or unlocked [@mayrhofer-android-security-model], [@hoog-android-forensics].

Reading the storage with a different device is generally impractical for routine verification and is often unsuitable in forensic contexts. If the device is physically damaged or unbootable, the storage is also unlikely to function reliably when transplanted. Even where specialized extraction is possible -- for example, chip-level desoldering and reading -- it demands equipment and expertise that most owners do not possess [@nist-sp800-101r1].

The available software paths are correspondingly limited. The platform's backup facility covers only applications that permit backup: it is enabled through the application manifest and applications that disable it are omitted from the resulting archive, and the mechanism is further bounded by per-application size limits and excluded directories [@aosp-auto-backup]. Approaches that would copy a full raw disk image to a computer do not work on devices that are neither rooted nor rootable, and comprehensive imaging of internal storage is generally described in the forensic literature as requiring root or an unlocked bootloader [@hoog-android-forensics].

These limitations are compounded by storage encryption, which is effectively universal on recent mainstream devices: file-based encryption has been mandatory on devices that launched with Android 10 or later, and has been available since Android 7 [@aosp-file-based-encryption]. The consequences of a hardware-bound encryption key are examined further in Section 11. Encryption is a second barrier behind the first, not the main one. The main obstacle to offline imaging is physical: for most owners the soldered chip cannot be removed and read at all, as described above. Where chip-level extraction does nonetheless succeed, encryption limits what it yields -- the user-data partition comes out as ciphertext, because the key that decrypts it is derived on-device from a secret bound to hardware-backed secure storage and cannot be reproduced off the device by routine extraction. The read-only system partitions are not encrypted this way and can be read, but they hold the vendor's code rather than the owner's data. The owner's own data is therefore doubly out of reach: the chip usually cannot be read, and on the rare occasion it can, that data is ciphertext. For most owners, therefore, inspection of internal storage is not reasonably achievable on a stock, non-rooted device.

## 8. Attestation and the Circular Trust Problem

Stock Android relies heavily on remote device attestation -- historically Google's SafetyNet Attestation API, which Google deprecated in 2022 and withdrew in January 2025 [@safetynet-attestation-deprecation], now replaced by the Play Integrity API [@play-integrity-overview] -- by which the device cryptographically reports aspects of its own state to applications and services. The reported state is grounded in the device's verified-boot status [@aosp-verified-boot] and backed by hardware-backed key attestation [@aosp-key-id-attestation]. Table 1 summarizes three simplified device-state claims that the platform exposes through its device-integrity signals; the Play Integrity API additionally returns separate application-integrity and account-integrity fields that are not reproduced here [@play-integrity-overview].

Table 1. Device attestation: what is reported to applications.

| Attestation claim | Meaning (simplified) |
| --- | --- |
| Running vendor-approved software | The device reports that it runs an operating system or build approved by the vendor, and often by Google. |
| Unmodified | The device reports that it has not been altered, for example that the bootloader has not been unlocked and the system has not been modified. |
| Locked down | The device reports that security-relevant settings remain enforced, including a locked boot chain and an expected integrity state. |

Attestation is a relying-party protocol, and naming its roles makes the direction of trust explicit. In the standard architecture the device is the Attester, which must produce evidence about itself; a Verifier appraises that evidence; and a Relying Party -- here the application developer, content provider, or platform operator -- is, in that architecture's words, the party that "depends on the validity of information about an Attester" before it will grant service [@rfc9334-rats]. Remote attestation is in general the activity of making a claim about a target's properties by supplying evidence to an appraiser over a network [@coker-remote-attestation]. The guarantee therefore runs to the relying party, not to the device owner: the device proves its compliance to a remote party that does not extend trust to the owner, and a passing verdict certifies the device to that party rather than giving the owner any means to audit or halt what an application does. That inversion -- software operating on the owner's own device while it is the owner who is kept from inspecting or stopping it -- is the loss of owner control named as the coming war on general-purpose computing [@doctorow-general-computation]. Accordingly, both "trusted status" and "attested status" in this paper denote this attested, vendor-approved standing that a remote party relies on, not any trust the owner places in the device. Android does surface a status to the owner -- the Play Store's Play Protect certification [@play-protect-certification] and the verified-boot warnings shown at startup [@aosp-boot-flow] -- but that status reports the platform's own approval verdict, reflecting a locked bootloader and a vendor-certified image; the owner must trust that verdict rather than independently audit it, and it is not a verification that the device is actually uncompromised: the device-integrity verdict attests boot and configuration state, not runtime malware-freeness [@play-integrity-verdicts], so a locked, certified device can still be compromised in ways this boot-time check does not cover -- malicious code shipped inside the certified image as pre-installed vendor software, as the Triada family was built into manufacturers' system images during production [@google-triada], or a runtime, memory-resident compromise of the already-booted and legitimately signed kernel. A persistent rootkit that instead rewrites the verified boot or system partitions is the one case verified boot and dm-verity are built to catch [@aosp-verified-boot]; Section 12 returns to the deeper breaches that can defeat even that.

This arrangement produces a circular trust dependency, summarized in Table 2. To inspect the system deeply, the owner must modify the device -- unlock the bootloader, obtain root, or install special builds -- which causes the device to fail attestation and to be treated as untrusted by applications. To remain trusted by applications, the owner must keep the device unmodified and locked down, which denies access to the disk, memory, logs, and low-level components that independent verification requires.

Table 2. Circular trust dependency: inspection versus attestation.

| Goal | What is typically required | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inspect the system deeply | Modify the device (unlock bootloader, gain root, use special builds) | Device may fail attestation and be treated as untrusted by applications |
| Remain trusted by applications | Keep the device unmodified and locked down | Limits access to the disk, memory, logs, and low-level components needed for independent verification |

From the platform's standpoint this is a security feature: it raises assurance that a device presenting itself to a service is in a known, unmodified state. From the standpoint of an owner facing a high-threat situation, the same mechanism is a verification dead-end, because the only configurations in which the device is considered trustworthy by the applications and services that demand it are precisely those in which the owner cannot examine it.

The dependency has, moreover, tightened over time, and now reaches beyond the owner's own verification into everyday use of the device. Attestation verdicts are tiered: the platform distinguishes a basic verdict from a device-integrity verdict and a stronger, hardware-backed verdict, and it exposes which tier a given device can satisfy [@play-integrity-improvements]. A device whose owner has unlocked the bootloader, installed an owner-controlled operating system, or obtained root typically cannot satisfy the device-integrity or strong verdicts, because those are tied to a Google-certified, vendor-signed configuration [@play-integrity-improvements], [@owasp-mastg-play-integrity]. A growing number of applications -- banking and payment applications, some enterprise and government applications, and content-protection components among them -- gate access on at least the hardware-backed device-integrity verdict, some additionally demanding the strong tier, and refuse to run, or silently degrade, on a device that cannot present them [@owasp-mastg-play-integrity].

The exact proportion of applications that do so is disputed and not rigorously measured, but the available evidence points firmly toward widespread and rising refusal. A code-level measurement reports that most banking applications contain no reference to Google's own attestation frameworks in their first-party code -- roughly nine in ten reference neither the Play Integrity API nor the deprecated SafetyNet, and the minority that do still overwhelmingly ship only SafetyNet calls [@macdonald-attestlens]. That figure is a floor on lock-out, not a measure of it: such a count detects only Google's own frameworks, and the same measurement observes that most attestation that is present originates from third-party packages rather than first-party code, so intentional adoption is systematically undercounted [@macdonald-attestlens]. The enforcement an owner actually encounters is delegated elsewhere -- to commercial application-shielding and runtime application self-protection (RASP) SDKs such as Promon SHIELD, Guardsquare/DexGuard, DexProtector, and Arxan, and to bespoke root, hook, and emulator checks that invoke no attestation API at all. A peer-reviewed systematization of mobile hardening finds these pervasive on current Android: root detection in 92.3 percent of apps, debugger detection in roughly 94 percent, and emulator detection in nearly 99 percent, with hardening most concentrated in the finance and shopping categories and, by that study's own account, driven largely by third-party libraries [@steinboeck-sok-hardening]. Consistent with this, crowd-sourced compatibility lists for owner-controlled operating systems catalog a long and growing set of banking and payment applications that refuse to run or install on them [@privsecdev-banking-grapheneos], [@grapheneos-attestation-compat]. The honest summary is therefore a floor-and-direction one: the first-party Play Integrity figure is a lower bound, the delegation of enforcement to RASP SDKs and non-attestation checks is why real-world refusal is substantially more common than that bound, and no single measurement yet fixes a precise lock-out rate. The owner, meanwhile, has no way to appeal a verdict except by returning the device to the vendor-approved state. The consequence for this paper's argument is structural: even where an owner is technically able to reach an owner-inspectable configuration, the attestation-and-hardening ecosystem imposes a rising functional cost for remaining there, so that the circular trust dependency is enforced not only by the platform's own refusal to trust a modified device but by the third-party software the owner must run on it.

This also sharpens what "gaining root" can and cannot achieve, a point developed in Sections 10 and 13. Because root is a bounded in-system privilege (Section 3) and because reaching it generally forfeits the attestation standing just described, obtaining root does not hand the owner the clean, external, trusted vantage point that independent verification requires; it trades the device's attested status for privileges that are themselves confined by mandatory access control, kernel lockdown, and the verified-boot chain. Modern rooting fits this bound rather than breaking it. The prevalent method is systemless -- rather than altering the /system partition it patches the partition image that holds the ramdisk, and the init the ramdisk carries, so it still requires an unlocked bootloader to reflash that image [@magisk-install]: the modification has moved down into the boot chain that verified boot protects, not out of its reach, and the device still forfeits the hardware-backed verdicts described above. The integrity-masking modules an owner adds alongside it spoof only the software-visible signals read by app-side root checks and the basic integrity verdict, not the device-integrity and strong verdicts that a locked bootloader and hardware key attestation anchor [@play-integrity-verdicts], [@grapheneos-attestation-compat], [@playintegrityfork]; forging those requires leaked, not-yet-revoked hardware key material of the kind Section 12 discusses, a key compromise rather than a defeat of the mechanism.

## 9. Detection of Low-Level Rootkits

Low-level rootkits conceal themselves by manipulating the operating system itself, so that security tools running on the same device may be unable to detect them reliably. Trusted detection can require obtaining a trusted snapshot of memory from outside the monitored operating system [@guri-joker-jtag].

The problem is structural rather than a matter of tool quality. Anti-virus software may successfully detect malicious applications yet remain ineffective against low-level rootkits that evade detection by masking their own presence [@guri-joker-jtag]. Any detection mechanism that runs on the same physical device as the monitored operating system can itself be compromised through application, kernel, or bootloader vulnerabilities, so trusted detection of kernel rootkits on mobile devices is, in practice, a challenging task [@guri-joker-jtag].

The distinction between user-space and kernel-space malware clarifies why. User-space malware can modify or inject code only into memory allocated to applications and user processes. Kernel-space malware can manipulate objects throughout the operating system's memory. Although mandatory access-control mechanisms such as SELinux are integrated into current Android versions, rootkits running at the operating-system level of mobile devices have been demonstrated [@bickford-rootkits-smartphones], [@brodbeck-covert-rootkit-detection]. A rootkit is kernel-space malware that uses illicitly obtained privileges to hide its existence by manipulating the kernel's internal data structures, and code that has penetrated kernel memory can neutralize any security tool running within the operating system. A request to the kernel for, say, the contents of a directory carries no guarantee of integrity in the returned list; consequently, detecting a rootkit requires a trusted snapshot of kernel memory obtained from outside [@guri-joker-jtag].

One proposed approach obtains such a snapshot through the JTAG hardware debugging interface rather than through the potentially compromised operating system. The approach exploits two debugging features: the ability to halt the processor instantly by issuing a special instruction, and the ability to read the device's volatile memory while it is halted. Because the acquisition system does not execute on the device, it can read kernel memory in a trusted manner; the captured memory is then processed by scripts that reconstruct kernel data structures and analyze them for suspicious modifications. The acquisition offers the advantage of trusted inspection precisely because it originates from outside the monitored system [@guri-joker-jtag].

For most owners and most devices, JTAG-based memory acquisition is not practical. Some devices expose a JTAG interface, but it is usually not accessible to typical owners; access tends to be device-specific and to require specialized equipment and skills. No quantitative data on the proportion of devices for which JTAG is usable has been located, and the situation is highly device-specific. Manufacturers may treat JTAG as an attack surface and disable or lock it on production hardware [@patent-secure-jtag], [@sguigna-jtag-attack-surface]: a recommended practice is to disable JTAG by default in production and end-user devices to reduce attack surface, enabling it only temporarily for authorized maintenance [@sguigna-jtag-attack-surface]. The same interface can nonetheless serve security analysis, and is an established forensic acquisition method for reading memory from embedded systems [@breeuwsma-jtag-imaging]. The approach's own authors acknowledge its awkwardness: it requires physical access to a JTAG port on the circuit board, which is cumbersome compared with software-based methods [@guri-joker-jtag], [@brodbeck-covert-rootkit-detection].

On many devices, deep investigation -- including the search for sophisticated rootkits -- requires root access, and where the bootloader cannot be unlocked, researchers may be driven to rely on exploitable vulnerabilities to gain privileges temporarily. Forensic analyses report that application sandboxing, encryption, and file-based access controls pose challenges to data acquisition [@nist-sp800-101r1], and that temporary-root techniques, which leverage operating-system vulnerabilities to obtain elevated privileges without permanently modifying the device, are used to obtain more comprehensive access including system-level files [@hoog-android-forensics].

For comparison, non-Android platforms such as desktop and server Linux provide suitable options for the same objectives. Anti-malware kernel modules designed to detect loadable-kernel-module rootkits, and runtime integrity guards, can run on the system [@thalium-rkchk], [@openwall-lkrg]. Alternatively, the system can be booted from external media and subjected to an offline integrity check using tools that verify installed files against a trusted manifest [@elstel-debcheckroot]. These options permit independent verification even where the original operating-system installation is itself suspected.

## 10. Malware-Analysis Capability Gaps

The constraints above translate into concrete gaps in analysis capability. This section contrasts a fully controlled general-purpose host, represented by traditional Linux desktops and servers, with a stock Android device.

On a traditional Linux host the examiner can replace the operating system, installing a known-good system or reinstalling from trusted media if compromise is suspected; boot from external media to obtain a clean, trusted environment without relying on the suspect system disk; inspect disks and memory from that trusted environment while minimizing reliance on the suspect operating system; instrument or replace the kernel to load debugging or integrity tooling; and run security tools outside the potentially compromised system, from an external host or trusted boot environment. Taken together, these capabilities permit independent verification even when the original installation is suspected to be malicious.

On a stock Android device these same capabilities are, in general, absent. The operating system cannot be freely replaced, so restoring a known-good system is usually unsupported or impractical. Booting an external, fully trusted operating system that bypasses the installed system is not supported. Full disk and memory access -- raw disk imaging, comprehensive file inspection, and live RAM capture -- is blocked without special privileges. Gaining those privileges usually breaks verified boot and attestation, causing the device to be treated as untrusted; can disable or degrade security features that depend on the device remaining locked down and unmodified; and can cause many applications, including banking, enterprise, and content-protected applications, to refuse to run. The net effect is that the tools needed to verify the system require breaking those same guarantees before they can be applied -- and, as Section 8 showed, the privileges so gained are themselves bounded and do not amount to the external trusted vantage point that verification needs.

Table 3 compares specific analysis capabilities. The values "Yes" and "No" indicate that a capability is, respectively, generally available or generally unavailable to the examiner on a stock device; "Partial" indicates that the capability exists only under significant constraints, typically requiring root, debugging permissions, or vendor or forensic access. The entries describe the device in its stock, as-sold configuration. An owner who unlocks the bootloader and installs an owner-controlled system can move several Android entries toward the Linux column, but only by forfeiting the attestation standing discussed in Section 8 and, with it, the ability to run the applications that depend on it; the comparison therefore characterizes the trade-off the owner faces on the device as sold, not an immutable property of the hardware.

| Aspect | Traditional Linux | Stock Android (as sold) |
| --- | --- | --- |

| User-controlled root | Yes -- commonly available in lab and test environments and on self-managed systems | No -- not available on the device as sold; typically requires bootloader unlock, custom builds, or an exploit |
| Raw disk imaging | Yes -- straightforward via standard forensic imaging workflows, including booting from external media | No -- not feasible on the device as sold; generally requires root, an unlocked bootloader, or specialized vendor or forensic access |
| Live memory acquisition | Yes -- readily possible with standard memory-capture tooling | No -- not feasible without root; no official RAM-acquisition interfaces |
| Kernel analysis | Yes -- kernel modules can be inserted, traced, or extracted; symbols often available | No -- unavailable on the device as sold without root, special builds, or vendor support |

## 11. Limitations on Encryption Key Backups

Backing up the device's encryption keys is not reasonably easy. Recent devices use file-based encryption, mandatory since Android 10 [@aosp-file-based-encryption]; the keys that protect user data are not held as ordinary files the owner can copy but are wrapped by a key bound to hardware-backed secure storage, such as a secure element or trusted execution environment, which places the protective key beyond the owner's reach. (Earlier devices used full-disk encryption, which likewise bound its master key to hardware rather than exposing it as a copyable file [@aosp-full-disk-encryption].) The use of a hardware-protected key is not in itself anomalous or an indication of a backdoor.

It is, rather, the normal arrangement in most Linux desktop distributions that offer full-disk encryption. In those systems the master key is itself stored on disk, and entering the passphrase at boot decrypts not the disk directly but the master key, which in turn decrypts the volume. The arrangement allows the passphrase to be changed without re-encrypting the entire volume, since only the stored master key must be re-wrapped. The difference on a stock device is that hardware protection of the key places it beyond the owner's reach rather than leaving it in a file the owner could back up.

Backing up the key may be possible on some devices if the device still boots and root can be obtained, but for most owners this is not straightforward.

## 12. Objections and Counterarguments

A principal objection holds that practical forensic tooling for mobile devices already exists and therefore contradicts the claim that stock devices resist verification. The Mobile Verification Toolkit (MVT), a consensual forensic tool intended to help analyze mobile devices for signs of spyware, is the usual example.

There is, however, no contradiction. Such a tool can identify known indicators of compromise using the data that the device exposes, but the stock mobile operating-system design limits that observability. The toolkit's own documentation states that Android devices provide much less observability than comparable iOS devices, that Android stores very little diagnostic information useful for triaging potential compromises, and that the toolkit's Android capabilities are correspondingly limited, while noting that not all is lost [@mvt-android-methodology]. Investigators are thus obliged to work with partial data and with known indicators of compromise, and a lack of findings is not definitive proof that a device was not compromised.

Independent forensic experience reinforces this. Investigating NSO Group's Pegasus spyware, Amnesty International's Security Lab reported that significantly more forensic traces are accessible to investigators on Apple iOS devices than on stock Android devices, and that its methodology was focused on iOS for that reason; as of that 2021 report, most of the then-recently confirmed Pegasus infections had involved iPhones [@amnesty-pegasus-methodology]. The report cautions that this does not by itself establish that iOS is more secure than Android. The same report characterized the difficulty of detecting attacks even after the fact as the result of an asymmetry between the capabilities available to attackers and the protections available to targeted individuals, observed that devices other than iOS provide little to no help for consensual forensic analysis, and encouraged device vendors to make their devices more auditable without sacrificing existing security and privacy protections [@amnesty-pegasus-methodology]. These observations support, rather than undermine, the conclusion that limited observability is a property of the platform design.

A second objection points to aftermarket Android-based operating systems -- GrapheneOS, CalyxOS, LineageOS, /e/OS, postmarketOS, and others -- as counterexamples that an owner can install in place of the vendor's build. They do not form a single case, and the differences are exactly what the owner-inspectability criterion measures. Most such systems are installed with the bootloader left unlocked, because the device cannot be re-locked against a key the vendor does not recognize, and an unlocked bootloader cuts both ways against the owner-inspectability criterion. It restores much of what the criterion's first half demands: the owner can boot an external trusted kernel or recovery image, image the partitions, capture live memory, and instrument the kernel -- the very capabilities Sections 9 and 10 found withheld on a stock device. What it gives up is the criterion's second half: without a locked chain the boot images are no longer cryptographically verified against a hardware root of trust on each startup, and although hardware key attestation still functions it can now report only an unverified boot state, not certify a locked, vendor-approved one, so a tamper of the boot chain between inspections is no longer prevented but at best detectable by hand. Such an installation therefore satisfies inspection while giving up the enforced, attestable verified-boot state -- an illustration of the squeeze this paper describes, not a counterexample to it. A smaller number support re-locking the bootloader after installation against a project-controlled or owner-controlled key, restoring verified boot under a non-vendor root of trust; among these, GrapheneOS is the furthest-developed case, as it additionally provides its own hardware-backed remote attestation by which an owner can verify from a second device that the system is the expected, untampered build running on a locked bootloader [@grapheneos-auditor]. It is therefore the strongest available approximation of an owner-inspectable device, and the right case on which to test the thesis.

Even there the limitations are relocated, not dissolved. The strongest guarantee is available on the project's own signed builds with the bootloader re-locked; an owner who instead builds and signs the system with a personal key obtains owner-controlled verified boot but steps outside any pre-established attestation relationship, so the "prove it to a skeptical third party" goal is met only against verifiers the owner has personally provisioned. Decisively for everyday use, such a configuration is not recognized as a Google-certified one, so it does not obtain the device-integrity or strong attestation verdicts that mainstream applications increasingly demand [@play-integrity-improvements], [@owasp-mastg-play-integrity]: the same owner-controlled posture that makes the device inspectable makes a growing set of applications refuse to run on it, and these projects' stated position is that they will not forge or spoof attestation results to impersonate a stock device. Aftermarket operating systems thus confirm rather than refute the paper's thesis: owner-inspectability is attainable, but only by leaving the as-sold, vendor-keyed configuration behind, after which the attestation ecosystem imposes the functional penalty described in Section 8. For the overwhelming majority of devices, which run their as-sold vendor software, the inspection limits catalogued here are unchanged.

One limit even this configuration cannot reach is that the owner still cannot independently image or re-verify every component the device runs. The cellular baseband is the clearest case: it executes its own vendor-controlled firmware on a separate processor that the application-processor operating system -- stock or aftermarket -- does not inspect, and remotely exploitable memory corruption in a baseband stack is a demonstrated class of attack [@weinmann-baseband]. A discrete secure element, where present, likewise runs vendor firmware on its own chip. The trusted execution environment should not be conflated with these: on a platform such as this one it shares the application processor and is signed and checked by the bootloader, so it falls under verified boot rather than being an uninspected separate processor; its limitation for the owner is that its firmware, like the baseband's, is not something the owner can independently image and audit, not that it runs unverified. The broader pattern is that persistent implants have migrated toward low-level firmware beneath the operating system, installable by ordinary malware once the system is compromised [@zaddach-hdd-backdoor]; the strongest owner-inspectable configuration still cannot independently audit that firmware layer, echoing in a new place the principle that a system cannot be trusted to report truthfully on its own state [@thompson-trusting-trust].

Verified boot is itself a strong defense but not an absolute one, and the owner cannot independently confirm that it holds. Its guarantees rest on assumptions -- that the signing keys stay secret, and that the bootloader and trusted execution environment enforcing the chain are themselves correct -- and these have been broken. Platform signing keys have leaked and been used to sign malware in the wild [@apvi-platform-certs], and bootloader and trusted-execution-environment compromises that would subvert the chain, including extraction of the hardware key material that underpins attestation, have been demonstrated in research and are reachable by malware that already controls the operating system, though none has been observed as an in-the-wild boot implant on a locked device [@bootstomp], [@shakevsky-trustzone]. Such malware cannot simply overwrite the bootloader -- the immutable hardware root of trust above it verifies the bootloader's signature and refuses a tampered image -- but it can exploit a flaw in the signed bootloader or trusted code to execute within the chain, which is how a software-only compromise reaches beneath the operating system. The hardware root of trust that anchors this check is itself a physical assumption rather than an absolute: the boot ROM is typically immutable mask ROM, so where it is a flaw in it is permanent and unpatchable, and glitching it can bypass signature verification altogether [@ncc-mediatek-bootrom] -- a class not specific to one vendor [@checkm8-vu]; some systems-on-chip instead expose a ROM-patch path: a small vendor-signed patch, held in one-time-programmable fuses, that redirects the boot ROM's early execution and is itself locked by a fuse [@opentitan-rom-patch]. Immutability is therefore a vendor design choice rather than a constant, and either way the mechanism is the vendor's and not the owner's; and being signed code gated by fuses, that patch path is itself subject to the same key-compromise and glitching failure modes, and adds early-boot attack surface of its own. An invasive silicon attack can further read out the fused device keys beneath the chain [@ioactive-antifuse]; but every such defeat documented to date requires physical possession of the device and belongs to the forensic and unlock class, not to any remote, in-the-wild implant on a locked consumer phone. A remote-seeded, locally-triggered variant -- malware or a malicious update planting malformed data that an early boot stage parses on the next boot -- is conceivable in principle, but none has been documented, and on a locked device the code the boot ROM loads is signature-verified against a fuse-anchored key [@aosp-verified-boot], so a purely over-the-air boot-ROM compromise is not a realistic or observed attack surface. The deeper point for the owner is epistemic: on the stock, vendor-keyed configuration the green boot state the device shows and the attestation certificate it presents are the platform's own self-report, which a sufficiently deep breach can forge and which the owner has no independent means to check. This limit is the hardware beneath the chain, not the verified-boot signing key above it: on devices that support it, Pixel among them, the owner can enroll a custom signing key and re-lock to it, reaching a yellow boot state whose on-screen key fingerprint the owner can themselves verify [@aosp-boot-flow], [@aosp-device-state] -- what stays beyond any owner is the boot ROM and the fused keys under that key, which none can replace or read out.

## 13. Discussion

The limitations examined here are not independent defects but facets of a single design choice: administrative capability is withheld from the device owner and conferred only on the vendor and manufacturer. Verified boot, sandboxing, mandatory access control, attestation, and hardware-bound key storage each advance the platform's assurance that only approved, unmodified software runs, and each simultaneously removes a means by which an owner might inspect the device from a trusted vantage point. The circular trust dependency of Section 8 is the sharpest expression of the tension: the configurations an owner would need in order to verify the device are exactly those the platform declines to trust.

The owner is consequently placed in a position analogous to that of an attacker with respect to introspection -- indeed a worse one, since these controls exclude the owner more reliably than they exclude the capable adversary of Sections 8 and 12 -- while retaining none of an attacker's willingness to damage the device. The practical recourses -- JTAG-based memory acquisition, chip-level storage extraction, and exploit-driven temporary root -- are, respectively, rarely available, destructive or specialized, and dependent on the existence of exploitable vulnerabilities that the platform is otherwise trying to eliminate. The physical-acquisition path is further foreclosed where unlocking the bootloader triggers a mandatory wipe of user data, so that gaining access to the storage also erases its contents [@aosp-bootloader-locking]. None of these recourses offers the routine, non-destructive, owner-driven verification that the threat model of Section 3 calls for, and, as Sections 3 and 8 established, even a successful temporary root yields only a bounded in-system privilege on a device that has already forfeited its attested standing -- not the external, trusted vantage point that verification requires. Nor would more privilege close the gap: a kernel-space rootkit manipulates the very kernel structures that any in-system observer, root included, must query, so it can neutralize a privileged security tool and falsify its results regardless of the privilege level at which that tool runs [@guri-joker-jtag], [@bickford-rootkits-smartphones]. Subversion-resistant detection must therefore observe the system from outside the running operating system [@guri-joker-jtag], [@garfinkel-vmi], echoing the long-established principle that a system cannot be trusted to report truthfully on its own state [@thompson-trusting-trust].

The contrast with a fully controlled host is one of category, not merely degree. On a desktop or server the baseline acquisition workflow -- detach the storage and image it through a write blocker, a device that passes read commands while blocking any that would alter the drive, so the acquisition does not modify the evidence [@nist-sp800-101r1], [@nist-hwb-spec], then decrypt the image with the owner's passphrase-derived key -- is available to essentially any owner, and remains so even though the storage device itself runs opaque controller firmware that a determined attacker can subvert: compromising the firmware of a commercial off-the-shelf drive to backdoor data as it is written has been demonstrated using only public information and reverse engineering, installable by ordinary malware once the operating system is compromised [@zaddach-hdd-backdoor], and such drive-firmware implants have been found in the wild [@kaspersky-equation], with persistence that survives disk formatting and a full operating-system reinstall [@kaspersky-equation-qa]. Implants one layer lower still, in the motherboard's own UEFI flash, persist even across a storage replacement, because they do not reside on the storage at all [@eset-lojax]. That residual is real, but it is a different thing from the phone's barrier: an advanced and partially mitigable attack vector layered beneath an acquisition path that otherwise works, not a wall that forecloses the path for every owner. On a stock phone the same baseline workflow is foreclosed at its first step, because the storage is soldered, and at its last, because even a successful chip-level read returns the user's data as ciphertext under a hardware-bound key. The firmware-implant concern, common to both platforms and marking the floor of what either owner can verify, thus compounds on the phone a barrier that is already categorical rather than standing, as on the host, as the one residual beneath an otherwise working path.

Hardware memory acquisition sharpens the same contrast, but only partly, because it is closing on both sides. On a conventional computer an examiner can in principle capture RAM from outside the running operating system -- by direct memory access over an expansion bus [@markettos-thunderclap], [@frisk-pcileech] or by exploiting the residual charge in DRAM after a cold reboot [@halderman-coldboot] -- yet modern platforms increasingly block such access with an input-output memory management unit [@markettos-thunderclap], and cold-boot capture needs physical access to the memory. A stock phone forecloses even this residual avenue: its memory is soldered in package-on-package stacks that cannot be transplanted, it exposes no external bus capable of such access, and the one demonstrated on-device cold-boot variant first required unlocking the bootloader, which wipes the user data it sought to recover [@muller-frost-coldboot].

Table 4 sets the owner's control over each layer of the stack side by side for the two platforms, read downward from the bootloader to the firmware beneath the operating system. The pattern is a categorical difference across the upper, operating-system-and-boot layers, where the personal-computer owner holds administrative control and the stock-phone owner holds none, and a shared opaque floor at the firmware and management-coprocessor layers below them, where neither owner can inspect what runs.

Table 4. Owner control over the stack: traditional personal computer versus stock Android (as sold).

| Stack layer | Traditional personal computer (owner) | Stock Android, as sold (owner) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bootloader | Replaceable; Secure Boot is optional, and the owner can enroll custom keys or disable it | Locked and vendor-signed; unlocking wipes user data and drops attestation, and some devices are unlockable only by exploit or not at all |
| Kernel | Owner builds and boots an arbitrary kernel | Vendor-signed boot image; a custom kernel requires unlocking |
| Kernel modules | Owner loads arbitrary modules | Lockdown, module-signature, and dm-verity enforcement restrict loading |
| initramfs | Owner controls | Part of the signed boot image; not owner-modifiable on stock |
| init (first userspace, PID 1) | Owner chooses or replaces the init system, and can boot to an alternate init or single-user mode | Android init ships in the signed ramdisk and system partitions under verified boot and dm-verity, runs under SELinux, and is not owner-modifiable on stock |
| Operating system | Owner installs any operating system | A custom operating system requires unlocking and incurs an attestation penalty |
| In-system superuser (root) | Available to the owner by default | Withheld as sold; reachable only by exploit or unlocking, and then bounded by SELinux and kernel lockdown |
| Boot an external trusted system | Yes, from external media | Not supported |
| Remove and image storage | Yes: detach the storage and image it on a trusted controller | Storage is soldered; chip-level reading is destructive, and the data returns as ciphertext under a hardware-bound key |
| Platform firmware | BIOS/UEFI: reflashable but vendor-signed and opaque in practice, and in-the-wild implants have been demonstrated [@eset-lojax] | Bootloader and vendor firmware: vendor-controlled and owner-opaque |
| Management co-processor | Intel ME or AMD PSP: a vendor-firmware co-processor the owner cannot inspect -- the ME embedded in the chipset with privileged access to platform data [@ermolov-intel-me], the AMD-SP a boot-time root of trust that runs before the main cores [@jacob-faultpm] | Cellular baseband, and any discrete secure element: vendor firmware on separate silicon the owner cannot inspect [@weinmann-baseband] |
| Storage-controller firmware | Opaque; controller-firmware implants demonstrated in the lab [@zaddach-hdd-backdoor] and found in the wild [@kaspersky-equation] | Opaque |

The division is systematic. On the personal computer the owner commands the entire operating-system-and-boot stack and is blocked only at the firmware and management-coprocessor floor; on the stock phone the owner commands essentially none of the stack, so the same firmware floor sits beneath a barrier that is already categorical at every layer above it. Administrative control, in the sense defined in Section 3, is exactly what the left column has and the right column lacks; root, where a phone owner reaches it, adds only the single "in-system superuser" row and leaves every other row unchanged.

The ease of this recourse is routinely overstated. The casual prescription to "just root it" presumes an act that retail devices are configured to resist: they ship with the bootloader locked, and unlocking it -- where the vendor permits it at all -- is gated behind an explicit warning and a mandatory wipe of user data [@aosp-bootloader-locking], [@aosp-boot-flow]. A large and well-documented class of carrier- and vendor-locked devices cannot be unlocked by their owners at all, or only by means of an exploit [@bootloader-wall-of-shame]. Unlocking is widely said to void the device warranty, although consumer-protection law in the United States and the European Union limits blanket voiding and requires a defect to be causally linked to the modification before coverage may be refused [@magnuson-moss-act], [@eu-directive-2019-771]. Several mainstream vendors attach irreversible hardware-level consequences to unlocking, by distinct mechanisms rather than a single universal one: one permanently trips a one-time-programmable e-fuse that disables hardware-backed attestation and the protected features that depend on it and cannot be reset [@samsung-knox-hw-security]; another irrevocably erases device-unique keys that relocking does not restore [@sony-unlock-bootloader]; and one-time-programmable (eFuse) hardware is, more generally, the standard system-on-chip primitive for secure-boot enablement and anti-rollback enforcement, whose blowing is a one-way operation [@qualcomm-secure-boot]. The honest counter-example is that not every platform is irreversible: on some devices unlocking can be reversed and the verified-boot and attestation state is restored on relocking -- to the full state under the vendor's stock key, or to a reduced state under an owner-enrolled key [@aosp-verified-boot], [@aosp-key-id-attestation]. Even there the squeeze holds, because the reversible path returns the owner to the vendor-keyed configuration without ever granting the top attestation standing while the device remains open to inspection. Taken together, these frictions make unlocking a high-barrier step rather than a routine one. The privileged state an owner does reach is, as Section 8 established, self-defeating for verification; and the cloaking tools owners deploy to pass integrity checks work only by impersonating a still-vulnerable stock device, leaving them locked in a steadily losing arms race against detection [@nguyen-vu-android-rooting].

The unlock procedure is, moreover, not silent to software already resident on the device. Preparing a device for inspection changes observable system state, and unlocking the bootloader triggers a mandatory wipe that destroys the user-data partition before any image of it can be taken -- so the inspection a high-threat owner attempts can itself alert, and then erase, a resident implant. That such environmental signals drive evasion is established rather than speculative: measurement studies show modern malware checks for virtualized or instrumented environments and withholds its behavior when it detects them [@chen-anti-virtualization], [@ruggia-evasive-malware], and the anti-forensics literature names the matching capabilities of artifact wiping, trail obfuscation, and self-removal [@garfinkel-anti-forensics], [@mitre-attack-evasion]. Mobile spyware has combined the two in the wild: NSO's Pegasus for Android shipped "suicide functionality to remove itself" [@lookout-chrysaor] (Suicide Functionality section), triggered among other cases when it cannot find a valid cellular subscriber identity -- behavior attributed to avoiding analysis and emulator environments -- while the iOS variant self-destructs when "a great probability of exposing the agent exists" [@marczak-million-dollar-dissident] (Section 3.5), and later versions were observed deleting their own process records to hide their traces [@amnesty-pegasus-methodology]. The specific loop -- an implant watching for unlock preparation and erasing itself before acquisition -- remains a reasoned extrapolation rather than a documented exploit; its constituent capabilities are each demonstrated, and the principle that a compromised system cannot be trusted to report truthfully on itself is long established [@thompson-trusting-trust], [@garfinkel-vmi]. The nearest realized case, the Rombertik malware, destroys its host on detecting that its own code has been analyzed [@talos-rombertik]; this is a genuine detect-then-destroy reaction, but it is keyed to tampering with the malware's own image rather than to a forensic acquisition, and it wrecks the host rather than quietly erasing the implant so the device passes inspection clean. No reported case closes the precise loop posited here.

### Limitations of this analysis

Several limitations bound the claims made here. The paper is a systematization of existing evidence, not a new empirical study: it reports no measurements of its own, and where the literature is thin -- notably on the precise prevalence of attestation-gated applications and on the fraction of devices for which hardware acquisition is feasible -- it says so rather than substituting an estimate. Its central construct, owner-inspectability, is a qualitative criterion rather than a metric; this paper defines and applies it but does not quantify it, and an operational scale by which devices and configurations could be scored is left to future work. The analysis is deliberately scoped to the owner's standpoint and to the Android platform as shipped on mainstream devices; it does not weigh the owner-side costs against the genuine ecosystem-wide benefits that the same mechanisms deliver against running adversaries, which the platform literature documents [@mayrhofer-android-security-model], and it does not extend its specific claims to other platforms except where a cited source does so. Finally, the platform is a moving target: verdict tiers, acquisition techniques, and project capabilities change between releases, and the specific mechanisms cited here are current as of writing rather than permanent.

A measured reading is that the platform optimizes for resistance to the ordinary running adversary at the cost of owner-side auditability, and that the two objectives, while not strictly incompatible, are in current designs rarely satisfied together. The forensic literature surveyed here does not claim that compromise is undetectable on stock devices; it claims that detection is constrained by observability, that negative results are weak evidence, and that auditability could be improved without abandoning existing protections. That position suggests a design space -- owner-attestable inspection modes, exportable integrity measurements, or supported trusted-boot verification paths -- in which verifiability and platform integrity might be advanced together rather than traded off.

## 14. Conclusion

Stock Android devices systematically limit the owner's ability to inspect, control, and independently verify the software they run. The owner cannot practically examine the whole system from a clean, trusted environment; application and update delivery is mediated by centralized providers whose abuse could enable targeted malicious installation; internal storage and live memory are inaccessible without withheld privileges; attestation couples a device's attested standing to the device remaining unmodified; low-level rootkit detection generally demands a trusted memory snapshot taken from outside the running system; and the established techniques of host-based malware analysis are, as a class, unavailable without first breaking the verified-boot and attestation guarantees the platform enforces. Obtaining root does not lift these limits, because root on a hardened device is a bounded in-system privilege -- not the full administrative control over the boot chain, kernel, and firmware that an owner would need -- and reaching it forfeits the attested status the owner is trying to preserve. Backing up the hardware-bound encryption key is likewise impractical for most owners. The existence of consensual forensic tooling, and of hardened aftermarket operating systems such as GrapheneOS, does not overturn these conclusions: such tooling is bounded by the limited diagnostic data the platform exposes, owner-inspectable configurations are reachable only by leaving the as-sold device behind and paying an attestation penalty to do so, and an absence of findings is accordingly weak evidence of integrity. Treating security as owner-side verifiability, rather than as platform-side policy enforcement, makes these limitations visible as a coherent whole and identifies owner-inspectability as the property most in need of improvement.

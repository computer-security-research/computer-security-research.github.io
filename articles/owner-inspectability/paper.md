# Limits of Independent Security Verification on Vendor-Locked Devices

## Abstract

Vendor-locked devices -- most prominently stock Android phones, which come with a locked bootloader and no administrative rights -- are general-purpose computers whose owners cannot fully inspect, control, or verify the software they run. This paper introduces the concept of owner-inspectability: the owner's ability to examine the whole system from a clean, trusted vantage point and demonstrate what it runs to a verifier of their choosing. It adopts the owner's perspective, treating security as the ability to audit the device rather than the platform's ability to enforce policy. Today, several limitations prevent this. The owner cannot inspect the system from a clean environment or directly access its storage and memory. Application and update delivery is vendor-controlled, so compromise or coercion could enable targeted malicious installations. Hardware-backed attestation lets apps and services check that the device reports an unmodified, vendor-approved state. Modifying it for inspection therefore makes the device fail attestation, causing banking, payment, and similar apps that depend on it to refuse to run. Root access does not fully remove these limitations: it provides only an in-system vantage point, not control over the boot chain and underlying firmware, and can be deceived by a kernel-space rootkit. The owner is caught in a bind: inspecting the device requires the very modification that attestation rejects. Most established malware-analysis techniques are thus unavailable without first breaking the phone's attestation guarantees, making the absence of findings weak evidence of integrity. The paper asks what capability would escape this bind: owner-controlled privileged inspection that preserves verified boot and attestation. It finds that no shipped or proposed approach provides this capability, leaving an open problem. The analysis focuses on stock Android but extends, to varying degrees, to other vendor-locked devices.

## 1. Introduction

On a vendor-locked device the owner is, in practice, obliged to trust the platform operator and device vendor, because the device cannot be fully examined from the outside while it remains locked down. On a stock Android phone that means trusting Google for application delivery through the Play Store [@google-play-help] and the device manufacturer for system updates. On a non-Google device such as a Samsung those updates are the manufacturer's own rather than Google's, though Google's services retain a privileged role in the update path on devices certified for Google Mobile Services [@aosp-sepolicy-gmscore] (Section 6). If that trust is abused -- through compromise of the delivery infrastructure, coercion of an operator, or a malicious insider -- targeted malicious installations or updates may be difficult to detect, independently verify, or prove to a third party.

The same constraints bear on malware investigation, though their force depends on the class of malware. Commodity, application-level threats can often be detected on the device itself, by consumer antivirus [@kaspersky-android] or Google's Play Protect [@google-play-protect], without unlocking or modifying it -- a point Section 12 takes up in full. This limitation is most acute for the targeted, low-level compromise: a kernel-space rootkit or sub-operating-system implant masks its own presence and sits beneath any in-system scanner, which it can evade whatever that scanner's privileges (Section 9). Against that class the owner typically cannot carry out the standard forensic approach -- examining the device from a separate, known-clean computer and working from a write-blocked image rather than the live original, as standard forensic practice directs [@nist-sp800-101r1], [@nist-sp800-86] -- without first unlocking or modifying the device. That modification can itself alter the evidence under examination, the mandatory wipe triggered by unlocking the bootloader being the extreme case [@aosp-bootloader-locking]; and a well-constructed backdoor could detect the onset of an investigation and erase itself or conceal its traces [@chen-anti-virtualization], [@ruggia-evasive-malware], [@garfinkel-anti-forensics], as Section 14 develops.

This paper treats these properties not as implementation accidents but as consequences of a design in which administrative capability is deliberately withheld from the owner. It catalogs the resulting verification limits, grounds its claims in the available technical and forensic literature where such sources exist, and distinguishes claims supported by cited evidence from claims that remain asserted without a specific source.

This is a systematization-and-position paper: it introduces no new experiment or dataset and instead organizes existing technical and forensic evidence around a single owner-centric criterion. Its contributions are:

- it names and defines owner-inspectability as the property an owner needs in order to audit a device, and distinguishes it from the related but distinct notion of organizational forensic readiness;
- it catalogs, against that criterion, the specific verification limits of stock Android and traces each to the same deliberate design choice rather than to isolated implementation defects;
- it shows that root access -- as distinct from the full administrative control withheld from the owner -- does not restore owner-inspectability on a modern hardened device, because root is only an in-system vantage point: it does not reach the kernel, firmware, and boot chain beneath it, it can be deceived by a kernel-space rootkit that falsifies what any in-system tool reports regardless of privilege, and reaching it forfeits the attestation standing the owner seeks to preserve;
- it documents how remote attestation, and the applications that depend on it, penalize owner-controlled configurations by a mechanism that has tightened over time, deepening the circular trust problem;
- it provides a structured capability comparison with a fully controlled host that makes the aggregate effect legible as a single design trade-off; and
- it frames owner-controlled privileged inspection -- a verified owner-inspection mode that preserves verified boot and relying-party attestation -- as the capability that would be needed to escape the inspection-versus-attestation bind, systematizes the design space of approaches shipped or proposed, and identifies the gap no current approach closes.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 summarizes the platform mechanisms. Section 3 states the threat model, scope, and terminology, including the constrained meaning of root and the definition of owner-inspectability. Section 4 situates the work in prior literature. Sections 5 through 11 catalog the verification limits. Section 12 addresses objections, including aftermarket operating systems. Section 13 asks whether a verified owner-inspection mode could escape the bind, and maps the design space of attempts. Section 14 discusses the findings and their limitations, Section 15 draws out the practical consequences through a real-world case, and Section 16 concludes.

## 2. Background

Android's security model is built around several mutually reinforcing mechanisms. Android Verified Boot (AVB) establishes a chain of trust from an immutable hardware root of trust through the bootloader and operating-system partitions, so that only software signed by the vendor executes during startup [@aosp-verified-boot]; a locked bootloader refuses to load or boot any image that is not so signed [@aosp-bootloader-locking]. Application sandboxing isolates each application, giving it a distinct kernel-level user identity and a private data directory that other applications cannot read [@aosp-app-sandbox]. Mandatory Access Control, implemented through SELinux, confines even privileged system processes to narrowly defined domains, so that a compromise of one component does not automatically yield access to the whole system [@aosp-selinux]. File-based encryption, mandatory since Android 10, protects user data at rest, with the principal encryption key bound to dedicated hardware [@aosp-file-based-encryption].

By design, these mechanisms constrain what ordinary software, including most malicious software, can do once it is running on the device [@mayrhofer-android-security-model]. That constraint is real but neither symmetric nor complete. A sufficiently capable adversary is not reliably excluded by it -- as Sections 8 and 12 develop, such an adversary can compromise the already-booted, legitimately signed system at runtime, or arrive inside the vendor-signed image itself, without the device failing attestation, and verified boot has demonstrated defeats beneath the operating system [@bootstomp], [@shakevsky-trustzone].

The same design, meanwhile, withholds from the device owner the administrative privileges that would permit raw disk access, live memory capture, kernel instrumentation, or the booting of an external trusted environment [@aosp-adb-root], [@vidas-android-collection]. The owner is therefore placed not in the same position as an attacker but, with respect to introspection, in a more constrained one, for a structural rather than a measured reason: the owner's only route to inspecting the device is the modification these protections are built to bar, whereas the capable adversary that the owner most fears need not modify the device at all and keeps the runtime, supply-chain, and sub-operating-system routes just described. These controls therefore foreclose the owner's only inspection route -- and that of a security researcher acting on the owner's behalf -- while leaving the runtime, supply-chain, and sub-operating-system routes of a capable adversary unaffected.

These mechanisms serve the owner's protection, but they also serve platform-control and relying-party objectives that the owner may not share. The platform works to keep unauthorized and malicious software off the device, through a layered apparatus, each layer of which covers a specific surface: verified boot and a locked bootloader admit only vendor-signed code to the boot and system partitions, keeping unauthorized code out of the system image though not out of the application layer [@aosp-verified-boot], [@aosp-bootloader-locking] (a chain that has itself demonstrated sub-operating-system defeats; Section 12); Google Play Protect screens apps and works to prevent the installation of harmful ones, with installation from unknown sources restricted by default [@google-play-protect], [@nistSP800124r2]; and the Play Integrity API gates relying-party access when a device appears tampered [@play-integrity-verdicts].

How well the app-layer screen catches malware is assessed here using independent testing results: in AV-Comparatives' May 2024 testing Play Protect reached a 99.3 percent protection rate, clearing the 99 percent certification threshold that round but trailing the leading third-party scanners at 99.9 percent and logging more false positives, an improvement over earlier independent rounds that had ranked it lowest, at well below 70 percent detection [@avcomparatives-mobile-2024], [@avtest-android-endurance-2018].

But the signed-image and attestation machinery also enforces objectives the owner may neither share nor be able to remove or audit -- that only a vendor-approved configuration runs, with application and service access gated by relying parties (the remote apps and services that consume the attestation verdict) rather than by the owner [@play-integrity-verdicts] -- so the guarantee runs in part to the platform and those relying parties, not to the owner alone. Such functionality, which the owner may not want, cannot remove, and cannot audit, has been termed an anti-feature in the free-software literature [@hill2007antifeatures], [@fdroid-antifeatures]; preinstalled applications, advertising components, and tracking software that the owner cannot uninstall without root are a documented instance [@gamba2020preinstalled]; Carrier IQ, preinstalled carrier diagnostic software that wrote dialer keypresses and SMS text to system logs on some devices, is an earlier one [@eckersley-carrieriq].

Content protection is the platform-control counterpart on the content side: on devices with the requisite hardware support, hardware-backed digital-rights-management can bind decryption and the decoded video frames to the same hardware chain of trust as verified boot, routing protected content through a trusted output path so that the owner may play licensed media but cannot directly extract the decoded stream, which is released only on terms the content provider sets [@aosp-drm]. This paper treats those platform-control and content-protection objectives as out of scope to analyze (Section 3) and returns to the question of which party the attestation guarantee serves (Section 8).

## 3. Threat Model and Scope

This paper defines security from the perspective of the end user and device owner: the ability to control, inspect, and independently verify the device. Under this definition a device is more secure when its owner can establish, from a trusted vantage point, whether it is running only the software they intend and what that software is doing -- free of malware or backdoors, whether planted by an outside attacker or by the platform itself -- and can produce evidence of that state that a skeptical third party could check.

Three practical capabilities are treated as the goals of interest: inspecting the device deeply for malware and backdoors; proving the result to a skeptical third party; and creating complete device backups -- a bit-for-bit image of the whole device, all partitions and user data, not the platform's limited app-level backup -- suitable for independent verification [@nist-sp800-101r1]. Proving the result of that deep examination to a skeptical third party is, on a general-purpose computer, the routine of forensic soundness: the examiner makes a write-blocked, bit-for-bit image of the storage and computes a cryptographic hash of it, which establishes the integrity of the evidence -- that the image is byte-identical to the original -- and makes the analysis reproducible on exactly those bits [@nist-sp800-86], [@rfc3227]. This does not by itself prove the device is clean; what it proves is that the skeptic need not trust the examiner's verdict, because the owner holds the published hash and a documented procedure and can hand any independent examiner the same verified bits to re-run the malware-and-backdoor analysis and reach their own conclusion.

Explicitly out of scope are digital-rights-management objectives and platform-control goals -- that is, the platform's interest in enforcing policy that constrains the owner [@doctorow-general-computation], in guaranteeing content protection [@aosp-drm], or in ensuring that only vendor-approved configurations run [@aosp-verified-boot], [@aosp-bootloader-locking]. These objectives frequently stand in tension with owner-centric verifiability, and conflating the two obscures the specific limitations examined here. Where the platform regards a property as a security feature and the owner regards the same property as an obstacle to verification, this paper describes it from the owner's standpoint and notes the platform's view where relevant.

Although this paper documents Android in detail as its worked example -- it is among the most openly specified of the mainstream vendor-locked platforms, the core of its system software released as the open-source Android Open Source Project (AOSP) [@aosp-open-source] -- that openness is a property of the source, not of the device as sold. AOSP is a codebase, not a complete operating system for real hardware: running it on a phone requires device-specific proprietary binaries the vendor distributes separately -- drivers, radio and baseband firmware, and hardware-abstraction blobs under non-open licenses [@google-driver-binaries], [@aosp-hal] -- and the mainstream "Google Android" product adds Google Mobile Services, a proprietary suite that is not part of AOSP and available only under license from Google [@google-gms]. The device as sold is therefore vendor-locked despite the open-source codebase beneath it, bundling proprietary components the owner cannot rebuild and shipping with the bootloader locked [@aosp-bootloader-locking] (Sections 8 and 12). The owner-inspectability criterion and the limits it exposes are not Android-specific. They follow from vendor-lock itself: a locked boot chain, administrative capability withheld from the owner, and attestation that ties the device's standing with apps and services to a vendor-approved state. By the same logic they apply, to varying degrees, to other vendor-locked devices, Apple iOS among them; the mechanisms differ in detail, but the owner's structural position does not. This paper references such platforms only where a cited source speaks to them and does not document them to the same depth.

Terminology is used consistently throughout. A device is vendor-locked, in the sense used here, when as sold it runs only vendor-signed system images under signing keys the vendor holds and the owner does not, with the bootloader locked -- so that the owner's only route to inspecting or replacing that software is to unlock the device, which, where the vendor permits it at all, triggers a mandatory wipe of user data [@aosp-bootloader-locking] and forfeits the verified-boot and hardware-backed attestation standing the device reports to software [@aosp-verified-boot] (Sections 8 and 12). The term names a structural position -- locked and vendor-keyed by default, the only exit being destructive and, for inspection, counterproductive -- not the narrower claim that unlocking is impossible: a Google Pixel permits unlocking yet is vendor-locked in this sense, while Apple iOS, which offers owners no unlock at all, is the limiting case. Stock Android denotes a typical device as sold, with a locked bootloader [@aosp-bootloader-locking] and without root access [@vidas-android-collection].

Root access denotes uid-0 authority within the running operating system: the ability, in principle, to read and write files, inspect processes, and alter system components that an ordinary user cannot. The term is inherited from Unix, where "root" is the superuser account; Android, however, exposes no such login account. uid 0 exists as a kernel identity held by `init` and a deliberately shrinking set of system daemons [@aosp-root-process-isolation], but a stock production ("user") build ships no owner-accessible `su` and refuses to run its debug bridge (`adbd`) as root [@aosp-adb-root], so the owner cannot assume that identity at all without first modifying the device.

Equally important is what this authority does not include on a modern hardened device, because the common assumption that root confers total control of the machine no longer holds. Root runs inside, and is bounded by, the platform's other defenses. Mandatory access control through SELinux confines even uid-0 processes to the domains the loaded policy permits, so that superuser identity gained within the already-booted system does not by itself grant unrestricted operations [@aosp-selinux]; the systemless root treated below escapes this confinement not by wielding more privilege within the booted system but by shaping the policy from beneath, as the boot image's `init` is patched before the policy is loaded.

The kernel's lockdown facility, once engaged, withdraws a range of otherwise root-accessible interfaces that could be used to modify the running kernel [@kernel-lockdown]. Module-signature enforcement can require that any code loaded into the kernel carry a valid signature, so that root cannot insert an arbitrary kernel module [@kernel-module-signing], and the `kernel.modules_disabled` control, once set, blocks all further module loading until reboot in a one-way fashion that even root cannot reverse [@kernel-modules-disabled].

Beneath the operating system, Android Verified Boot and a locked bootloader anchor the boot chain to a hardware root of trust and refuse unsigned images, so that root -- an in-system privilege -- cannot by itself rewrite the firmware or boot chain that sit below it [@aosp-verified-boot], [@aosp-bootloader-locking]. Throughout this paper, therefore, "root" names a powerful but bounded in-system privilege, not unconstrained control of the kernel, firmware, or boot chain; Section 10 and the discussion in Section 14 return to why obtaining it still does not deliver owner-inspectability.

One caveat attaches to the mechanisms just named: Android's Generic Kernel Image does not enforce a valid signature on every loadable kernel module -- module signing is not supported for GKI vendor modules, and on devices that require verified boot the authenticity of kernel modules rests instead on dm-verity over the read-only partitions that hold them [@aosp-loadable-kernel-modules] rather than on per-module signature enforcement. The mechanisms above are capabilities of the platform, deployed to differing degrees across devices, not uniform guarantees on every device.

Administrative control, by contrast, denotes the broader authority an owner traditionally holds over a general-purpose computer: the ability to replace the bootloader, kernel, kernel modules, initramfs, and operating system, and to boot an external trusted environment of the owner's choosing, with uid-0 inside a system so constituted. On a traditional personal computer this authority over the operating-system and boot stack is the owner's by default; on stock Android it is withheld from the owner and conferred only on the vendor. Root, when an owner obtains it at all, is a fragment of this authority -- superuser standing that observes the system only from within it, without command of the kernel and boot chain beneath it, and in its prevalent systemless form obtained by patching `init` rather than held as an account inside the booted system -- which is why, as Sections 8, 10, and 14 develop, obtaining root does not amount to administrative control and does not by itself deliver owner-inspectability. Section 14 contrasts the two stacks directly, including the firmware layers that remain opaque to the owner on both.

A clean, trusted environment denotes a separate computer or externally booted system under the examiner's control, as opposed to the device's own currently running operating system.

Owner-inspectability is the central property this paper isolates. A device is owner-inspectable when its owner can examine and verify the whole system -- storage, memory, and the boot and operating-system state -- from a clean, trusted vantage point, and can establish and demonstrate what the device is running, both to themselves and to any verifier they choose to trust. The criterion is owner-facing: it does not require the device to also keep the vendor-approved attested status on which a remote relying party, not the owner, depends. That vendor-approved standing is a property in tension with owner-inspectability rather than a part of it -- on stock Android the two are coupled, so that gaining owner inspection forfeits the vendor-approved status, and it is that bind, not any inability to self-verify, that this paper traces.

The term is deliberately distinct from forensic readiness as defined in the digital-forensics literature, which concerns an organization's advance preparation to collect and preserve admissible evidence about its own systems and users [@rowlingson-forensic-readiness], [@iso-27043]: forensic readiness is a posture an institution arranges for investigators, whereas owner-inspectability is a property the device either affords its owner or withholds. Owner-inspectability is also narrower than a general right to modify or repair a device: it requires that the owner be able to establish, and to demonstrate, what the device is running -- not that the device be freely modifiable. That separation is conceptual: in practice the two entangle, since establishing what a locked device runs -- most of all its live, whole-system state -- can itself require modifying it (Section 14).

That separation is clean only for persistent, at-rest state: inspecting the files, installed packages, and boot chain a device holds at rest is a read-only act that requires no modification. Detecting non-persistent, memory-resident malware is different, because such code leaves no durable artifact and can be observed only through live memory acquisition or runtime and kernel instrumentation [@sudhakar-fileless-malware], which on a locked device is itself a modification -- unlocking, rooting, or booting a custom kernel [@yang-live-memory-acquisition] -- that, as Section 8 shows, forfeits the device's attested status. For such live threats inspectability and modifiability cannot be cleanly separated, and the owner's bind deepens rather than eases. AOSP in principle allows a device to be locked to a user-controlled root of trust rather than only to the vendor's [@aosp-verified-boot]; whether that latent capability is usable in practice, and whether it survives contact with the attestation ecosystem, is examined in Sections 8 and 12.

## 4. Related Work

The platform mechanisms examined here are documented authoritatively in the Android platform security model, which sets out verified boot, application sandboxing, mandatory access control, and attestation as a coherent design and states the threat model they address [@mayrhofer-android-security-model]. That account is written from the platform's standpoint -- the device defending itself, its users' data, and the ecosystem against a running adversary. The present paper adopts the complementary standpoint of the owner who wishes to audit the device, and reads the same mechanisms for what they deny that owner.

Three bodies of prior work supply the evidence reorganized here. The mobile-forensics literature establishes how, and how far, data can be acquired from Android devices: official guidance on sound acquisition and its preconditions [@nist-sp800-101r1], empirical studies of imaging and rooting-based acquisition [@son-android-data-integrity], [@vidas-android-collection], and field reports on the limited traces recoverable from stock devices in spyware investigations [@amnesty-pegasus-methodology], [@mvt-android-methodology]. The rootkit-detection literature establishes that trustworthy detection of kernel-level compromise requires a vantage point outside the running operating system, and explores hardware-assisted acquisition to obtain one [@guri-joker-jtag], [@bickford-rootkits-smartphones], [@brodbeck-covert-rootkit-detection]. The anti-forensics and evasion literature establishes that malicious code routinely detects analysis environments and removes its own traces [@chen-anti-virtualization], [@ruggia-evasive-malware], [@garfinkel-anti-forensics].  Each cluster is examined in detail in the sections that follow; the contribution of this paper is not new measurement within any one of them but their systematization around owner-inspectability.

The owner-inspectability criterion relates to, but is distinct from, organizational forensic readiness [@rowlingson-forensic-readiness], [@iso-27043] and from remote-attestation design [@mayrhofer-android-security-model], [@play-integrity-improvements]; the distinctions are drawn where each is used. To the author's knowledge, the limits of owner-side verification on stock Android have not previously been assembled into a single, criterion-driven account.

Two further strands bear directly on the objections in Section 12. The security cost of rooting as actually deployed has been measured: a study of the commercial one-click-root ecosystem found that root providers distribute kernel exploits that malware can equally abuse, so root as shipped tends to enlarge the device's attack surface rather than give the owner controlled privileged access [@zhang-root-providers]. And the tension between owner control and remote attestation has a prior line of remedy: property-based attestation proposes certifying that a platform satisfies required security properties rather than that it runs one specific binary configuration, which would let an owner-modified system attest without being locked to a vendor image [@sadeghi-property-attestation]; the owner-signed configuration examined in Section 12 is a concrete instance of the same impulse, bounded in practice by attestation roots the owner does not control.

## 5. Independent Verification Limits

On most stock devices it is difficult to independently verify the state of the whole system. When malware, a hidden backdoor, or tampering is suspected, the owner generally cannot examine the system from a clean, trusted environment without first modifying the device (Section 10). The owner is therefore left to trust the vendor-controlled software and services that deliver applications and updates, even though those channels could themselves be abused in a targeted manner (Section 6).

The underlying cause is architectural. Android is commonly shipped as a locked-down general-purpose computer in which administrative capability is deliberately withheld from the owner and the bootloader is locked. Many protections and integrity checks are implemented under vendor control, limiting what the owner can change, inspect, or replace. As a result the owner is typically prevented from inspecting the whole system and from producing a complete device backup without first bypassing these restrictions.

These restrictions bear on the three goals defined above (Section 3). First, deep inspection for malware and backdoors is impeded: on a non-rooted device, the private files of other applications are inaccessible, and system areas and logs are access-controlled rather than openly readable [@mayrhofer-android-security-model], [@aosp-selinux], which makes it harder to confirm whether suspicious behavior originates from malware or from a normal system component, and harder to rule out targeted tampering (Section 10). Second, the owner cannot readily prove a clean or compromised result to a third party, because the evidence that would support such a proof cannot be collected. Third, a complete device backup -- for example, a full offline image of the relevant partitions -- generally requires privileges the device restricts (Section 7); on a non-rooted device "full backup" in practice means a partial backup mediated by the operating system and by individual applications [@android-allowbackup], [@aosp-auto-backup].

For the same reasons, demonstrating the presence or absence of a hidden backdoor is difficult [@mvt-android-methodology], [@amnesty-pegasus-methodology]. An examiner who cannot fully inspect system files, logs, and low-level components without first modifying the device is restricted to what the running operating system reports -- the same limited vantage point a well-concealed backdoor would rely on (Section 9).

## 6. Risks of Centralized Application and Update Delivery

Because key parts of application and update delivery are controlled by the platform operator and device vendor -- on Android, Google Play for applications and the manufacturer for system firmware -- vendor-locked devices are exposed to the risk of targeted malicious application installations, application upgrades, and system upgrades.

A documented and ordinary feature illustrates the mechanism. Many devices permit a user to sign in to the Google Play web or desktop store using the same Google account, typically the same email address, that is used on the device. Selecting "Install" for an application in the web interface prompts the user, where multiple devices are registered, to choose a target device; the application is then installed on that device. This demonstrates that Google Play can initiate an installation to a specific, linked device without direct physical interaction with that device [@google-play-help].

The concern is not that an arbitrary outsider can invoke this capability without authorization. The concern is that control of the delivery system confers significant capability. If Google Play's infrastructure were compromised, or if a privileged insider were malicious or coerced, this capability could in principle be abused to push a backdoored version of an application to a specific target. By the same logic, device manufacturers control firmware and system updates, so targeted malicious system upgrades are likewise possible if that control is abused.

These are not merely hypothetical concerns about the delivery architecture: the capability it concentrates has repeatedly been the actual vector of a backdoor elsewhere, and has been the explicit object of legal compulsion. A backdoor, in the standard sense, is an undocumented or hidden means of access that bypasses a system's normal authentication or security controls, whether an unintentional flaw, a mechanism deliberately installed by the system's creator, or one surreptitiously installed by an intruder [@rfc4949]. The trusted code-signing channel that underpins software updates has been abused in exactly this way: the Flame malware obtained a certificate that chained to the Microsoft root authority and could therefore sign code trusted across Windows [@flame-collision], and a change to one of the elliptic-curve points used by the Dual EC generator planted a passive traffic-decryption capability in Juniper's ScreenOS firmware [@juniper-dual-ec]. Both were third-party abuses of a signing or firmware channel rather than vendor-planted trapdoors, but each shows the choke point is exploitable.

The same channel has also been the object of legal compulsion: a United States court ordered a vendor to produce and cryptographically sign custom firmware loadable onto a specific device [@apple-order-2016] (an order later withdrawn once the government obtained access by other means), and Australia has legislated compulsory technical-capability notices that can require a provider to build a new capability, including a new decryption capability, into its product [@tola-2018] (the statute's prohibition on building a "systemic weakness" is contested, because it excludes a weakness "selectively introduced to one or more target technologies that are connected with a particular person"). The security literature names the structural hazard directly: exceptional access "would create concentrated targets that could attract bad actors," and "complexity is the enemy of security" [@keys-under-doormats]. A national cybersecurity authority has raised the kindred concern for trusted-computing hardware, cautioning that running a system on a TPM 2.0 can entail a loss of owner control over the operating system and the hardware, and that the mechanisms involved could be turned to sabotage by a third party [@bsi-tpm-2013]. The point for owner-inspectability is deliberately narrow: a mandatory, un-refusable, cryptographically gated update channel, combined with hardware attestation, concentrates into a single vendor-controlled choke point the capability to deliver code the owner can neither refuse nor independently inspect. That is a backdoor vector -- a capability a compromised or compelled vendor could exploit -- not evidence that any such backdoor exists in a given product; what the owner provably lacks is the means to establish that it does not.

United States foreign-intelligence law illustrates how far such compulsion reaches, and where it stops. Under Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act a provider can be directed to "immediately provide the Government with all information, facilities, or assistance necessary to accomplish the acquisition" (50 U.S.C. 1881a(i)) [@usc-50-1881a], a directive the government may enforce by petitioning to compel compliance, on pain of contempt. That authority has been used to compel assistance and the disclosure of cryptographic keys: a provider was ordered to produce "all information necessary to decrypt communications ... including encryption keys and SSL keys," enforced by a five-thousand-dollar-per-day sanction (In re Under Seal, 749 F.3d 276 (4th Cir. 2014)) [@lavabit-4cir-2014], and another was compelled to assist warrantless acquisition over its objection, reportedly under a threat of escalating daily fines (In re Directives, 551 F.3d 1004 (FISA Ct. Rev. 2008)) [@inre-directives-2008]. Whether the authority extends further -- to compelling a vendor to build a new backdoor into its product -- is legally untested: the only litigated attempt to compel affirmative engineering, the order discussed above, arose under the All Writs Act rather than FISA and was withdrawn without a ruling [@apple-order-2016]; no published order construes the Section 702 assistance clause to mandate product redesign; and the Communications Assistance for Law Enforcement Act forbids the government to "require any specific design of equipment, facilities, services, features, or system configurations" (47 U.S.C. 1002(b)(1)) [@usc-47-1002], though that bar's reach over a Section 702 directive is itself unadjudicated. National Security Letters, by contrast, reach only non-content records and cannot compel a capability at all (18 U.S.C. 2709) [@usc-18-2709]. Because these instruments operate under nondisclosure, the absence of a public example settles nothing about what has or has not been ordered. Scholarly analysis of United States compelled-access law reaches the same division: it treats compelled provider assistance and key disclosure as established, court-sanctioned mechanisms, while finding the authority to compel a manufacturer to affirmatively weaken or redesign its own product "largely unresolved" [@kerr-schneier-2018].

Update-related communication is, moreover, not anonymous on devices certified for Google Mobile Services (GMS). Google Play services periodically checks in with Google, transmitting persistent hardware identifiers -- the IMEI, hardware serial number, network MAC address, and Google Services Framework identifier -- that Google documents as associated with the signed-in Google account and used, among other purposes, to determine a device's eligibility for software updates and security patches [@leith-handset-privacy], [@google-device-config-service]. Whether a particular firmware update is delivered through Google or through the device vendor varies by manufacturer. The AOSP security policy itself grants Google Play services access to the system-update service and to the A/B update engine, confirming its privileged role in the update path on such devices [@aosp-sepolicy-gmscore]. The data Google Play services sends to Google in this way is not something the user can opt out of while the service is in use: an independent measurement study of GMS-certified handsets found that Google Play services and the Play store transmit a considerable volume of data to Google even when the device is idle and privacy settings are enabled, and reports that Google confirmed there is no opt-out from this collection [@liu-android-oem-snooping].

Network-level anonymization does not neutralize this exposure, because the exposure is at the identity layer rather than the network layer. Using Google Play on a stock Android device requires the device to be signed in to a Google account; the store cannot be browsed or used to install applications without one [@google-play-help]. Every interaction with Google Play authenticates that account to Google, so each install or update request is bound to a specific, persistent identity. Routing the device's traffic through Tor conceals the network (IP) address from which a request originates, and in that narrow sense remains functional; but the account-based authentication required by the operating system still presents the user's Google identity to Google on every request. The network layer is thus anonymized while the identity layer is not. An account identifier is not, by definition, the user's legal identity, but on a stock Android device it is in practice strongly linkable to one: account creation commonly requires phone verification, which Google documents as a check that the holder is a real person [@google-verify-account], and a phone number is itself bound to a registered identity under the near-ubiquitous mandatory SIM-registration regimes [@donovan-sim-registration]. The account also accumulates an extensive, identity-linked record of activity and device identifiers over time [@schmidt-google-data-collection], and the network origin from which it is used remains recoverable from provider-side logs, whose retention many jurisdictions have mandated [@eu-data-retention-directive]. Presenting the signed-in Google account on each request therefore discloses an identifier strongly linkable to the user, so routing the device's traffic through Tor conceals the network origin without conferring requester anonymity.

## 7. Limitations of Internal Storage Access on Non-Rooted Devices

Accessing internal storage for purposes such as data recovery or malware and rootkit analysis presents significant practical barriers on most stock devices. The guiding principle of malware analysis and backdoor detection is that a device suspected of compromise should not be relied upon to examine itself [@rfc3227]; the data should ideally be inspected from a separate, known-clean computer, in keeping with established forensic guidance on sound acquisition and evidence preservation [@nist-sp800-101r1]. Stock Android prevents exactly this.

The obstruction has two roots. First, internal storage is typically a soldered component that cannot be removed without specialized work [@nist-sp800-101r1]. Second, Android normally blocks access to many system files and to the private files of other applications unless the device is rooted or unlocked [@mayrhofer-android-security-model], [@son-android-data-integrity].

Reading the storage with a different device is generally impractical for routine verification and is often unsuitable in forensic contexts. If the device is physically damaged or unbootable, the storage is also unlikely to function reliably when transplanted. Even where specialized extraction is possible -- for example, chip-level desoldering and reading -- it demands equipment and expertise that most owners do not possess [@nist-sp800-101r1].

The available software paths are correspondingly limited. The platform's backup facility covers only applications that permit backup: it is enabled through the application manifest and applications that disable it are omitted from the resulting archive, and the mechanism is further bounded by per-application size limits and excluded directories [@aosp-auto-backup]. Approaches that would copy a full raw disk image to a computer do not work on devices that are neither rooted nor rootable, and comprehensive imaging of internal storage is generally described in the forensic literature as requiring root or an unlocked bootloader [@son-android-data-integrity], [@vidas-android-collection].

These limitations are compounded by storage encryption, which is effectively universal on recent mainstream devices: file-based encryption has been mandatory on devices that launched with Android 10 or later, and has been available since Android 7 [@aosp-file-based-encryption]. The consequences of a hardware-bound encryption key are examined further in Section 11. Encryption is a second barrier behind the first, not the main one. The main obstacle to offline imaging is physical: for most owners the soldered chip cannot be removed and read at all, as described above. Where chip-level extraction does nonetheless succeed, encryption limits what it yields -- the user-data partition comes out as ciphertext, because the key that decrypts it is derived on-device from a secret bound to hardware-backed secure storage and cannot be reproduced off the device by routine extraction. The read-only system partitions are not encrypted this way and can be read, but they hold the vendor's code rather than the owner's data. The owner's own data is therefore doubly out of reach: the chip usually cannot be read, and on the rare occasion it can, that data is ciphertext. For most owners, therefore, inspection of internal storage is not reasonably achievable on a stock, non-rooted device.

## 8. Attestation and the Circular Trust Problem

Stock Android relies heavily on remote device attestation -- historically Google's SafetyNet Attestation API, which Google deprecated in 2022 and withdrew in January 2025 [@safetynet-attestation-deprecation], now replaced by the Play Integrity API [@play-integrity-overview] -- by which the device cryptographically reports aspects of its own state to applications and services. The reported state is grounded in the device's verified-boot status [@aosp-verified-boot] and backed by hardware-backed key attestation [@aosp-key-id-attestation]. Table 1 summarizes three simplified device-state claims that the platform exposes through its device-integrity signals; the Play Integrity API additionally returns separate application-integrity and account-integrity fields that are not reproduced here [@play-integrity-overview].

Table 1. Device attestation: what is reported to applications.

| Attestation claim | Meaning (simplified) |
| --- | --- |
| Running vendor-approved software | The device reports that it runs an operating system or build approved by the vendor, and often by Google. |
| Unmodified | The device reports that it has not been altered, for example that the bootloader has not been unlocked and the system has not been modified. |
| Locked down | The device reports that security-relevant settings remain enforced, including a locked boot chain and an expected integrity state. |

Attestation is a relying-party protocol, and naming its roles makes the direction of trust explicit. In the standard architecture the device is the Attester, which must produce evidence about itself; a Verifier appraises that evidence; and a Relying Party -- here the application developer, content provider, or platform operator -- is, in that architecture's words, the party that "depends on the validity of information about an Attester" before it will grant service [@rfc9334-rats]. Remote attestation is in general the activity of making a claim about a target's properties by supplying evidence to an appraiser over a network [@coker-remote-attestation].

The guarantee therefore runs to the relying party, not to the device owner: the device proves its compliance to a remote party that does not extend trust to the owner, and a passing verdict certifies the device to that party rather than giving the owner any means to audit or halt what an application does. That inversion -- software operating on the owner's own device while it is the owner who is kept from inspecting or stopping it -- has been characterized as a broader loss of owner control over general-purpose computing [@doctorow-general-computation]. Accordingly, both "trusted status" and "attested status" in this paper denote this attested, vendor-approved standing that a remote party relies on, not any trust the owner places in the device.

Android does surface a status to the owner -- the Play Store's Play Protect certification [@play-protect-certification] and the verified-boot warnings shown at startup [@aosp-boot-flow] -- but that status reports the platform's own approval verdict, reflecting a locked bootloader and a vendor-certified image; the owner must trust that verdict rather than independently audit it, and it is not a verification that the device is actually uncompromised: the device-integrity verdict attests boot and configuration state, not runtime malware-freeness [@play-integrity-verdicts], so a locked, certified device can still be compromised in ways this boot-time check does not cover -- malicious code shipped inside the certified image as pre-installed vendor software, as the Triada family was built into manufacturers' system images during production [@google-triada], or a runtime, memory-resident compromise of the already-booted and legitimately signed kernel, such as the in-the-wild Binder use-after-free in the Android kernel that escalated to full device compromise [@projectzero-badbinder].

These runtime compromises are documented, not hypothetical: commercial surveillance vendors sell turnkey exploit chains that defeat current, fully patched devices, and Google's Threat Analysis Group attributes about half of known zero-day exploits against Google and Android targets to such vendors [@tag-buying-spying], in one case observing a full chain fired against an up-to-date Samsung phone running the latest Chrome [@tag-cytrox]; Project Zero maintains the running in-the-wild record [@projectzero-itw]. Once resident, such an implant operates inside the running system's own trust boundaries -- the Predator spyware's loader injects into zygote to assume other application identities and move between SELinux domains [@talos-predator] -- so the application sandbox and SELinux confinement of Section 2 restrain it far less than they restrain ordinary software.

A persistent rootkit that instead rewrites the verified boot or system partitions is the one case verified boot and dm-verity are built to catch [@aosp-verified-boot]; Section 12 returns to the deeper breaches that can defeat even that.

This arrangement produces a circular trust dependency, summarized in Table 2. To inspect the system deeply, the owner must modify the device -- unlock the bootloader, obtain root, or install special builds -- which causes the device to fail attestation and to be treated as untrusted by applications. To remain trusted by applications, the owner must keep the device unmodified and locked down, which denies access to the disk, memory, logs, and low-level components that independent verification requires.

Table 2. Circular trust dependency: inspection versus attestation.

| Goal | What is typically required | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inspect the system deeply | Modify the device (unlock bootloader, gain root, use special builds) | Device may fail attestation and be treated as untrusted by applications |
| Remain trusted by applications | Keep the device unmodified and locked down | Limits access to the disk, memory, logs, and low-level components needed for independent verification |

From the platform's standpoint this is a security feature: it raises assurance that a device presenting itself to a service is in a known, unmodified state [@play-integrity-verdicts]. From the standpoint of an owner facing a high-threat situation, the same mechanism forecloses independent verification, because the only configurations in which the device is considered trustworthy by the applications and services that demand it are precisely those in which the owner cannot examine it.

The dependency has, moreover, tightened over time, and now reaches beyond the owner's own verification into everyday use of the device. Attestation verdicts are tiered: the platform distinguishes a basic verdict from a device-integrity verdict and a stronger, hardware-backed verdict, and it exposes which tier a given device can satisfy [@play-integrity-improvements]. A device whose owner has unlocked the bootloader, installed an owner-controlled operating system, or obtained root typically cannot satisfy the device-integrity or strong verdicts, because those are tied to a Google-certified, vendor-signed configuration [@play-integrity-improvements], [@owasp-mastg-play-integrity]. Some applications -- banking and payment applications and content-protection components among them -- gate access on at least the hardware-backed device-integrity verdict, some additionally demanding the strong tier, and refuse to run, or silently degrade, on a device that cannot present them [@owasp-mastg-play-integrity], [@play-integrity-verdicts], [@aosp-drm], [@privsecdev-banking-grapheneos].

The exact proportion of applications that do so is disputed and not rigorously measured. The available evidence is limited and indirect, but the measurements that exist are consistent with refusal being common and increasing. A code-level measurement reports that most banking applications contain no reference to Google's own attestation frameworks in their first-party code -- roughly nine in ten reference neither the Play Integrity API nor the deprecated SafetyNet, and the minority that do still overwhelmingly ship only SafetyNet calls [@macdonald-attestlens]. That figure is a floor on lock-out, not a measure of it: such a count detects only Google's own frameworks, and the same measurement observes that most attestation that is present originates from third-party packages rather than first-party code, so intentional adoption is systematically undercounted [@macdonald-attestlens].

The enforcement an owner actually encounters is delegated elsewhere -- to commercial application-shielding and runtime application self-protection (RASP) SDKs such as Promon SHIELD, Guardsquare/DexGuard, DexProtector, and Arxan, and to bespoke root, hook, and emulator checks that invoke no attestation API at all. A peer-reviewed systematization of mobile hardening finds these pervasive on current Android: root detection in 92.3 percent of apps, debugger detection in roughly 94 percent, and emulator detection in nearly 99 percent, with hardening most concentrated in the finance and shopping categories and, by that study's own account, driven largely by third-party libraries [@steinboeck-sok-hardening]. Consistent with this, crowd-sourced compatibility lists for owner-controlled operating systems catalog a set of banking and payment applications that refuse to run or install on them [@privsecdev-banking-grapheneos], [@grapheneos-attestation-compat].

The summary is therefore a floor-and-direction one: the first-party Play Integrity figure is a lower bound, the delegation of enforcement to RASP SDKs and non-attestation checks is why real-world refusal is substantially more common than that bound, and no single measurement yet fixes a precise lock-out rate. The owner, meanwhile, has no way to appeal a verdict except by returning the device to the vendor-approved state. The consequence for this paper's argument is structural: even where an owner is technically able to reach an owner-inspectable configuration, the attestation-and-hardening ecosystem imposes a rising functional cost for remaining there, so that the circular trust dependency is enforced not only by the platform's own refusal to trust a modified device but by the third-party software the owner must run on it.

This also sharpens what "gaining root" can and cannot achieve, a point developed in Sections 10 and 14. Because root is an in-system privilege (Section 3) and because reaching it generally forfeits the attestation standing just described, obtaining root does not hand the owner the clean, external, trusted vantage point that independent verification requires; it trades the device's attested status for an in-system foothold that still observes the kernel only from within it -- and, where lockdown or module-signature enforcement is engaged, cannot even load code into that kernel.

Modern rooting fits this bound rather than breaking it. The prevalent method is systemless: it gains root without writing to `/system`. Verified boot covers `/system` (guarded by a dm-verity hashtree [@aosp-dm-verity]) and the boot and `init_boot` images (an AVB hash in the signed `vbmeta`) alike, so on a locked bootloader neither can be altered; the unlock is the shared precondition, after which the device boots an unsigned image and Magisk disables `vbmeta` verification [@aosp-verified-boot], [@magisk-install]. It patches the `init` carried in the ramdisk -- in the boot partition, the `init_boot` partition on devices launched with Android 13 or later, or, on some devices, `vendor_boot` or a recovery image [@aosp-partitions], [@magisk-install] -- because `init` is the earliest userspace hook, then overlays its changes at runtime, leaving the read-only `/system` byte-identical: official OTAs stay applicable (root is re-applied to the updated slot afterward, not carried across) and the change is reversible by reflashing the stock boot image [@magisk-install], [@magisk-ota]. Modern "root" is in this sense better described as control of `init` than as an in-system account: the patched `init` runs before the SELinux policy is loaded, so the installer shapes the policy its own daemon then runs under -- an unconstrained, permissive domain confined to that daemon and the root shells it grants, while every other domain stays enforcing [@magisk-details] -- rather than being confined by it.

Even so, this foothold is not the external vantage point verification needs. It is the administrative-tier power to replace the initramfs (Section 3), reached only through the unlock that forfeits verified boot, and the permissive domain sidesteps SELinux but not the running kernel beneath it: loading code into that kernel is blocked where lockdown, module-signature enforcement, or the one-way module-disable control are engaged [@kernel-lockdown], [@kernel-module-signing], [@kernel-modules-disabled], though not uniformly -- the Generic Kernel Image does not enforce per-module signatures (Section 3) -- and in any case this root observes the kernel only from within it, so a kernel-space rootkit beneath it falsifies what any in-system tool reports regardless of privilege (Sections 9 and 14).

A clean, out-of-system vantage comes not from the ramdisk patch but from the same unlock's further use -- booting a separate kernel or recovery to inspect the partitions at rest -- and even that leaves the suspect kernel's live memory beyond trusted reach (Section 14). The modification has thus moved down into the boot chain that verified boot protects, not out of its reach, and the device still forfeits the hardware-backed verdicts described above.

The integrity-masking modules an owner adds alongside it spoof the software-visible signals read by app-side root checks and the basic verdict, and on devices launched before Android 13 -- whose device-integrity verdict is the weaker successor to SafetyNet's `ctsProfileMatch` -- they can pass that verdict too by rewriting `android.os.Build` fields and system properties, with no hardware key material [@playintegrityfork]. What software spoofing cannot forge is the hardware-backed device-integrity verdict that devices launched with Android 13 and later enforce through locked-bootloader checks, nor the strong verdict: passing those requires genuine certified hardware with a locked bootloader, or leaked, not-yet-revoked hardware key material of the kind Section 12 discusses [@play-integrity-verdicts], [@play-integrity-improvements], [@grapheneos-attestation-compat], [@playintegrityfork] -- a key compromise rather than a defeat of the mechanism.

## 9. Detection of Low-Level Rootkits

Low-level rootkits conceal themselves by manipulating the operating system itself, so that security tools running on the same device may be unable to detect them reliably. Trusted detection can require obtaining a trusted snapshot of memory from outside the monitored operating system [@guri-joker-jtag].

The problem is structural rather than a matter of tool quality. Anti-virus software may successfully detect malicious applications yet remain ineffective against low-level rootkits that evade detection by masking their own presence [@guri-joker-jtag]. Any detection mechanism that runs on the same physical device as the monitored operating system can itself be compromised through application, kernel, or bootloader vulnerabilities, so trusted detection of kernel rootkits on mobile devices is, in practice, a challenging task [@guri-joker-jtag].

The distinction between user-space and kernel-space malware clarifies why. User-space malware can modify or inject code only into memory allocated to applications and user processes. Kernel-space malware can manipulate objects throughout the operating system's memory. Although mandatory access-control mechanisms such as SELinux are integrated into current Android versions, rootkits running at the operating-system level of mobile devices have been demonstrated [@bickford-rootkits-smartphones], [@brodbeck-covert-rootkit-detection]. A rootkit is kernel-space malware that uses illicitly obtained privileges to hide its existence by manipulating the kernel's internal data structures, and code that has penetrated kernel memory can neutralize any security tool running within the operating system. A request to the kernel for, say, the contents of a directory carries no guarantee of integrity in the returned list; consequently, detecting a rootkit requires a trusted snapshot of kernel memory obtained from outside [@guri-joker-jtag].

One proposed approach obtains such a snapshot through the JTAG hardware debugging interface rather than through the potentially compromised operating system. The approach exploits two debugging features: the ability to halt the processor instantly by issuing a special instruction, and the ability to read the device's volatile memory while it is halted. Because the acquisition system does not execute on the device, it can read kernel memory in a trusted manner; the captured memory is then processed by scripts that reconstruct kernel data structures and analyze them for suspicious modifications. The acquisition offers the advantage of trusted inspection precisely because it originates from outside the monitored system [@guri-joker-jtag].

For most owners and most devices, JTAG-based memory acquisition is not practical. Some devices expose a JTAG interface, but it is usually not accessible to typical owners; access tends to be device-specific and to require specialized equipment and skills. No quantitative data on the proportion of devices for which JTAG is usable has been located, and the situation is highly device-specific. Manufacturers may treat JTAG as an attack surface and disable or lock it on production hardware [@patent-secure-jtag], [@sguigna-jtag-attack-surface]: a recommended practice is to disable JTAG by default in production and end-user devices to reduce attack surface, enabling it only temporarily for authorized maintenance [@sguigna-jtag-attack-surface]. The same interface can nonetheless serve security analysis, and is an established forensic acquisition method for reading memory from embedded systems [@breeuwsma-jtag-imaging]. The approach's own authors acknowledge its practical limitations: it requires physical access to a JTAG port on the circuit board, which is cumbersome compared with software-based methods [@guri-joker-jtag], [@brodbeck-covert-rootkit-detection].

On many devices, deep investigation -- including the search for sophisticated rootkits -- requires root access, and where the bootloader cannot be unlocked, researchers may be driven to rely on exploitable vulnerabilities to gain privileges temporarily. Forensic analyses report that application sandboxing, encryption, and file-based access controls pose challenges to data acquisition [@nist-sp800-101r1], and that rooting -- commonly achieved by executing an exploit against an operating-system vulnerability, or by booting a custom recovery mode that grants administrative privileges while minimizing alteration of stored user data -- provides the access needed for fuller acquisition [@son-android-data-integrity], [@vidas-android-collection].

For comparison, non-Android platforms such as desktop and server Linux provide options for the same objectives. Anti-malware kernel modules designed to detect loadable-kernel-module rootkits, and runtime integrity guards, can run on the system [@thalium-rkchk], [@openwall-lkrg], though in-system monitors remain subject to the same kernel-level evasion described in Section 9. Alternatively, the system can be booted from external media and subjected to an offline integrity check using tools that verify installed files against a trusted manifest [@elstel-debcheckroot]. These options permit independent verification even where the original operating-system installation is itself suspected.

## 10. Malware-Analysis Capability Gaps

The constraints above translate into concrete gaps in analysis capability. This section contrasts a fully controlled general-purpose host, represented by traditional Linux desktops and servers, with a stock Android device.

On a traditional Linux host the examiner can replace the operating system, installing a known-good system or reinstalling from trusted media if compromise is suspected; boot from external media to obtain a clean, trusted environment without relying on the suspect system disk; inspect disks and memory from that trusted environment while minimizing reliance on the suspect operating system; instrument or replace the kernel to load debugging or integrity tooling; and run security tools outside the potentially compromised system, from an external host or trusted boot environment. Taken together, these capabilities permit independent verification even when the original installation is suspected to be malicious.

On a stock Android device these same capabilities are, in general, absent. The operating system cannot be freely replaced, so restoring a known-good system is usually unsupported or impractical. Booting an external, fully trusted operating system that bypasses the installed system is not supported on the device as sold: while the bootloader is locked it requires a valid signature before it will boot or install any image, so an external, unsigned system cannot be started without first unlocking it [@aosp-fastboot-protocol], [@aosp-bootloader-locking]. Even on an unlocked device there is no universal external-boot path -- starting an external image is vendor- and device-specific, and cumbersome even where it works at all [@bootloader-wall-of-shame]. Full disk and memory access -- raw disk imaging, comprehensive file inspection, and live RAM capture -- is blocked without special privileges. Gaining those privileges usually breaks verified boot and attestation, causing the device to be treated as untrusted; can disable or degrade security features that depend on the device remaining locked down and unmodified; and can cause many applications, including banking, enterprise, and content-protected applications, to refuse to run. The net effect is that the tools needed to verify the system require breaking those same guarantees before they can be applied -- and, as Section 8 showed, the privileges so gained are in-system, not the external, trusted vantage point that verification needs, and no amount of in-system privilege supplies one.

Table 3 compares specific analysis capabilities. The values "Yes" and "No" indicate that a capability is, respectively, generally available or generally unavailable to the examiner on a stock device; "Partial" indicates that the capability exists only under significant constraints, typically requiring root, debugging permissions, or vendor or forensic access. The entries describe the device in its stock, as-sold configuration. An owner who unlocks the bootloader and installs an owner-controlled system can move several Android entries toward the Linux column, but only by forfeiting the attestation standing discussed in Section 8 and, with it, the ability to run the applications that depend on it; the comparison therefore characterizes the trade-off the owner faces on the device as sold, not an immutable property of the hardware.

Table 3. Comparison of malware-analysis capabilities: a traditional Linux host the owner administers versus stock Android (as sold).

| Aspect | Traditional Linux | Stock Android (as sold) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| System access (examiner-controlled) | Yes -- full root access common in testbeds and forensic imaging | No -- root typically restricted; requires manufacturer unlock (often unavailable) or an exploit to gain privileged access |
| User-controlled root | Yes -- commonly available in lab and test environments and on self-managed systems | No -- not available on the device as sold; typically requires bootloader unlock, custom builds, or an exploit |
| Raw disk imaging | Yes -- straightforward via standard forensic imaging workflows, including booting from external media | No -- not feasible on the device as sold; generally requires root, an unlocked bootloader, or specialized vendor or forensic access |
| Live memory acquisition | Yes -- readily possible with standard memory-capture tooling | No -- not feasible without root; no official RAM-acquisition interfaces |
| Kernel analysis | Yes -- kernel modules can be inserted, traced, or extracted; symbols often available | No -- unavailable on the device as sold without root, special builds, or vendor support |
| Kernel integrity verification | Yes -- possible using external boot media and host-controlled integrity tooling | No -- not available in a user-controlled way; verification is typically limited and vendor-controlled |
| Independent integrity verification | Yes -- possible by booting a trusted environment and performing independent integrity checks | No -- limited on the device as sold; attestation and integrity guarantees are typically vendor-controlled |
| Binary reverse engineering | Yes -- straightforward access to malware files, system binaries, and logs | Partial -- possible, but often limited to application-level packages unless rooted or firmware access is available |
| Hook and injection analysis | Yes -- library preloading, process tracing, and syscall interposition easily observed | Partial -- requires rooting; SELinux and sandboxing block injection methods by default |

| External monitoring | Yes -- broad options, including out-of-band approaches and host-controlled capture and analysis | No -- limited on the device as sold; out-of-band monitoring is generally unavailable without elevated access or specialized tooling |
| Log-based forensics | Yes | Partial -- limited; absence of traces after thorough manual analysis does not establish that a device was not compromised, and log-based methods may, where compromise is strongly suspected, need to be combined with other forensic techniques [@socialtic-log-forensics] -- which divide by vantage point: off-device network-traffic monitoring stays feasible without touching the locked device, whereas the full-image, live-memory, and trusted-boot inspection of Sections 7, 9, 10, and 13 are exactly the capabilities a locked device withholds |

## 11. Limitations on Encryption Key Backups

Backing up the device's encryption keys presents significant practical barriers. Recent devices use file-based encryption, mandatory since Android 10 [@aosp-file-based-encryption]; the keys that protect user data are not held as ordinary files the owner can copy but are wrapped by a key bound to hardware-backed secure storage, such as a secure element or trusted execution environment, which places the protective key beyond the owner's reach. (Earlier devices used full-disk encryption, which likewise bound its master key to hardware rather than exposing it as a copyable file [@aosp-full-disk-encryption].) The use of a hardware-protected key is not in itself anomalous or an indication of a backdoor.

The underlying arrangement -- a master key that decrypts the volume, itself unlocked by the user's passphrase -- is the normal one in Linux full-disk encryption. There the master key is stored on disk wrapped by the passphrase, so entering the passphrase at boot decrypts the master key, which in turn decrypts the volume. The arrangement allows the passphrase to be changed without re-encrypting the entire volume, since only the stored master key must be re-wrapped [@luks-cryptsetup-faq]. The difference on a stock device is that hardware protection of the key places it beyond the owner's reach rather than leaving it in a file the owner could back up.

Backing up the key may be possible on some devices if the device still boots and root can be obtained, but for most owners this is not straightforward.

## 12. Objections and Counterarguments

A principal objection holds that practical forensic tooling for mobile devices already exists and therefore contradicts the claim that stock devices resist verification. The Mobile Verification Toolkit (MVT), a consensual forensic tool intended to help analyze mobile devices for signs of spyware, is the example considered here.

There is, however, no contradiction. Such a tool can identify known indicators of compromise using the data that the device exposes, but the stock mobile operating-system design limits that observability. The toolkit's own documentation states that Android devices provide much less observability than comparable iOS devices, that Android stores very little diagnostic information useful for triaging potential compromises, and that the toolkit's Android capabilities are correspondingly limited, while noting that several forensic artifacts remain accessible [@mvt-android-methodology]. Investigators are thus obliged to work with partial data and with known indicators of compromise [@mvt-android-methodology], and a lack of findings is not definitive proof that a device was not compromised.

Independent forensic experience reinforces this. Investigating NSO Group's Pegasus spyware, Amnesty International's Security Lab reported that significantly more forensic traces are accessible to investigators on Apple iOS devices than on stock Android devices, and that its methodology was focused on iOS for that reason; as of that 2021 report, most of the then-recently confirmed Pegasus infections had involved iPhones [@amnesty-pegasus-methodology]. The report cautions that this does not by itself establish that iOS is more secure than Android [@amnesty-pegasus-methodology]. The same report characterized the difficulty of detecting attacks even after the fact as the result of an asymmetry between the capabilities available to attackers and the protections available to targeted individuals, observed that devices other than iOS provide little to no help for consensual forensic analysis, and encouraged device vendors to make their devices more auditable without sacrificing existing security and privacy protections [@amnesty-pegasus-methodology]. The same report also notes that where the spyware no longer persists, its payload is not recoverable from the non-volatile filesystem and can be obtained only from volatile memory -- by jailbreaking the device without reboot -- so an examination confined to stored artifacts cannot surface it, which is the in-memory, kernel-level case of Section 9 [@amnesty-pegasus-methodology]. These observations are consistent with limited observability being a property of the platform design.

A second objection points to aftermarket Android-based operating systems -- GrapheneOS, CalyxOS, LineageOS, /e/OS, postmarketOS, and others -- as counterexamples that an owner can install in place of the vendor's build. They do not form a single case, and the differences are exactly what the owner-inspectability criterion measures.

Most such systems are installed with the bootloader left unlocked, because the device cannot be re-locked against a key the vendor does not recognize, and an unlocked bootloader cuts both ways against the owner-inspectability criterion. It restores much of what the criterion's first half demands: the owner regains privileged, at-rest inspection a stock device withholds -- imaging the partitions above all, and, on well-supported hardware, booting an owner-controlled kernel or recovery to instrument the system -- the capabilities Sections 9 and 10 found absent. This reaches less far in practice than in principle: a fully functional owner-built kernel is confined to a minority of devices, because the vendor hardware-abstraction interface [@aosp-hal], the generic-kernel module boundary [@aosp-gki], [@aosp-loadable-kernel-modules], and non-rebuildable firmware blobs tie working hardware to the vendor's own code; and the suspect system's live memory stays out of reach, since live acquisition runs in the already-booted kernel, so booting one's own kernel first reboots the device and discards the volatile state that soldered memory already places beyond trusted external capture (Section 14).

What it gives up is the criterion's second half: without a locked chain the boot images are no longer cryptographically verified against a hardware root of trust on each startup, and although hardware key attestation still functions it can now report only an unverified boot state, not certify a locked, vendor-approved one, so a tamper of the boot chain between inspections is no longer prevented but at best detectable by hand. Such an installation therefore satisfies inspection while giving up the enforced, attestable verified-boot state -- an illustration of the bind this paper describes, which the owner-inspectability criterion is designed to capture.

A smaller number of them support re-locking the bootloader after installation [@grapheneos-install-locking], [@grapheneos-setup-wizard], and this is where the owner-inspectability criterion discriminates most finely, because the re-locked chain can be anchored to either of two very different keys. Anchored to the project's own key -- the default when an owner flashes official GrapheneOS release builds, which replace the vendor's verified-boot key in the device's secure element with GrapheneOS's own [@aosp-device-state], [@grapheneos-install-locking] -- re-locking restores verified boot but only substitutes one vendor's root of trust for another's: the signing key is now GrapheneOS's rather than Google's, and the owner still does not hold it. On the key-control half of the criterion this official-build configuration is the stock position under a different vendor's key, not owner control of the boot chain; replacing an official Google image with an official GrapheneOS image changes the vendor, not the owner's standing. Even if Google allowlisted such a build so that it passed the hardware-backed integrity verdicts that banking and payment apps gate on, that would be the attestation ecosystem and its relying parties choosing to accept GrapheneOS as trusted, not the owner inspecting or verifying the device.

GrapheneOS is one case on which the thesis can be tested, because it adds what the stock platform withholds from the owner: owner-accessible attestation tooling -- the Auditor app, which verifies, from a second device or on a schedule against an attestation server the owner can run themselves [@grapheneos-auditor], [@grapheneos-attestation-server], that the system is the expected, untampered build running on a locked bootloader, built on the standard hardware attestation primitive but packaged as a verify-to-yourself capability rather than a relying-party one.

The configuration that actually meets the key-control half is the other anchoring: an owner who builds GrapheneOS from source, signs it with keys they generate and keep, and re-locks to their own enrolled verified-boot key holds a root of trust they control [@grapheneos-build]; tooling exists to produce such owner-signed builds from signed over-the-air updates without a full source rebuild, though only on supported Pixel hardware, and it too documents that no owner-signed configuration can satisfy the hardware-backed Play Integrity verdict [@resign-android-image].

GrapheneOS's own Auditor app, in its released form, nonetheless does not recognize that owner key: its attestation accepts only official GrapheneOS keys in the self-signed verified-boot state -- an alternate OS re-locked under a non-stock key -- because an alternate system's verified-boot key must be compiled into the released app and server to be recognized [@grapheneos-auditor], [@grapheneos-auditor-source], [@grapheneos-auditor-usecase] (Appendix A), so verifying an owner-signed build needs a verifier the owner configures with their own key -- an owner-built Auditor and, if remote checking is wanted, an owner-run attestation server. That owner-signed path is the configuration that actually satisfies the owner-inspectability criterion for the main system -- the owner both holds the root of trust and can verify the device against it, to themselves or to any verifier they provision -- and is the configuration this paper uses to test the thesis.

Even there one limitation is relocated rather than dissolved, and it is worth naming precisely. The owner-signed configuration delivers verified boot under the owner's own key and an attestation the owner can check -- to themselves, or to any verifier willing to adopt that key as a trust anchor -- so for the owner's own purposes, establishing and demonstrating what the device runs, both halves of the criterion are met. What it cannot do is prove itself to a verifier that refuses in advance to trust the owner's key: hardware attestation still demonstrates genuine hardware in a locked, self-signed boot state to anyone who checks, but a skeptic has no published, independent anchor for the owner's verified-boot key, so the gap is the absence of a trust anchor, not the unavailability of the key -- publishing the key would not close it. An official GrapheneOS key differs only in that a relying party can choose to trust GrapheneOS, a public project with a reproducible build [@grapheneos-build], as that anchor; a self-generated key vouches for no one but its owner. For everyday use, neither the owner-signed build nor the official GrapheneOS build is a Google-certified configuration, so neither obtains the device-integrity or strong attestation verdicts that mainstream applications increasingly demand [@play-integrity-improvements], [@owasp-mastg-play-integrity]: the same owner-controlled posture that makes the device inspectable makes an apparently growing set of applications refuse to run on it, and GrapheneOS's stated position is that it will not spoof or otherwise circumvent these checks to impersonate a stock device [@grapheneos-usage].

Other owner-controlled systems take the opposite tack: the microG-based distributions CalyxOS and /e/OS bundle a reimplementation of Google Play Services that depends on signature spoofing to impersonate those services to the apps that call them [@microg-gmscore], [@calyxos-microg], [@eos-microg], and rooted owners add integrity-masking modules -- Universal SafetyNet Fix and the Play Integrity Fix family, optionally paired with a keybox provider such as Tricky Store -- that spoof the attestation signals themselves [@safetynet-fix], [@playintegrityfork], [@tricky-store]. Signature spoofing of this kind restores access to Google's APIs, not a passing attestation: microG obtains at most the software basic-integrity verdict, while the hardware-backed device and strong verdicts require a certified device with a locked bootloader and stay out of reach [@eos-microg], so the banking- and payment-application refusals of Section 8 persist on these systems -- CalyxOS's own guidance states that compatibility depends on the specific app and that apps requiring full Play Services integration, such as Google Pay, will still not work [@calyxos-banking]. That spoofing path is itself narrow and shrinking: as Section 8 describes, only the basic and legacy pre-Android-13 verdicts fall to software spoofing -- Universal SafetyNet Fix states that "Android versions up to 13 are supported" and has been unmaintained since 2023 [@safetynet-fix] -- while the hardware-backed verdict on devices launched with Android 13 or later yields only to a leaked attestation keybox, and those are not durable, publicly shared keys being banned by Google as they surface [@playintegrityfork], [@tricky-store]; it is the arms race Section 14 describes, in which the spoofing side has been progressively constrained [@nguyen-vu-android-rooting].

That bind raises a further question -- whether any owner-controlled privilege could escape it rather than merely confirm it, a verified owner-inspection mode that preserves both verified boot and attestation -- which Section 13 takes up, systematizing the design space and the gap none of its approaches closes.

Aftermarket operating systems are thus consistent with the paper's analysis: owner-inspectability is attainable, but only by leaving the as-sold, vendor-keyed configuration behind, after which the attestation ecosystem imposes the functional penalty described in Section 8. For the overwhelming majority of devices, which run their as-sold vendor software, the inspection limits catalogued here are unchanged.

One limit even this configuration cannot reach is that the owner still cannot independently image or re-verify every component the device runs. The cellular baseband is the clearest case: it executes its own vendor-controlled firmware on a separate processor that the application-processor operating system -- stock or aftermarket -- does not inspect, and remotely exploitable memory corruption in a baseband stack is a demonstrated class of attack [@weinmann-baseband]. On several Samsung Galaxy models the proprietary radio-interface-layer software on the application processor -- the vendor library that mediates the modem -- was moreover found to implement a remote-file-service protocol through which the modem can read, write, and delete files on that storage; its finders characterized this as a backdoor, though whether it was a deliberate backdoor rather than an incidental feature of the modem's file service is not established [@replicant-samsung-backdoor]. More recent analysis of Samsung devices continues to uncover undisclosed baseband command interfaces and vendor-confirmed zero-day flaws, including denial of cellular service and arbitrary access to the application processor's file system from the baseband [@li-basemirror]. The Samsung finding was itself obtained by reverse-engineering that proprietary vendor library, a reminder that realizing inspectability in practice can draw on the modify-and-reverse-engineer freedoms held conceptually distinct from it in Section 3, and can carry legal exposure the owner or researcher must navigate: the finders rested the lawfulness of their decompilation on the interoperability exception in Article 6 of the EU Computer Programs Directive [@replicant-samsung-backdoor-wiki], [@eu-computer-programs-directive].

A discrete secure element, where present, likewise runs vendor firmware on its own chip. The trusted execution environment should not be conflated with these: on a platform such as this one it shares the application processor and is signed and checked by the bootloader, so it falls under verified boot rather than being an uninspected separate processor; its limitation for the owner is that its firmware, like the baseband's, is not something the owner can independently image and audit, not that it runs unverified. The broader pattern is that persistent implants have migrated toward low-level firmware beneath the operating system, installable by ordinary malware once the system is compromised [@zaddach-hdd-backdoor]; the strongest owner-inspectable configuration still cannot independently audit that firmware layer, echoing in a new place the principle that a system cannot be trusted to report truthfully on its own state [@thompson-trusting-trust].

A third objection holds that verified boot and hardware key attestation already furnish the integrity assurance that owner-inspection is meant to provide, so the owner has no need to verify the device independently. Verified boot is itself a strong defense but not an absolute one, and the owner cannot independently confirm that it holds. Its guarantees rest on assumptions -- that the signing keys stay secret, and that the bootloader and trusted execution environment enforcing the chain are themselves correct -- and these have been broken. Platform signing keys have leaked and been used to sign malware in the wild [@apvi-platform-certs], and bootloader and trusted-execution-environment compromises that would subvert the chain, including extraction of the hardware key material that underpins attestation, have been demonstrated in research and are reachable by malware that already controls the operating system, though none has been observed as an in-the-wild boot implant on a locked device [@bootstomp], [@shakevsky-trustzone]. Such malware cannot simply overwrite the bootloader -- the immutable hardware root of trust above it verifies the bootloader's signature and refuses a tampered image -- but it can exploit a flaw in the signed bootloader or trusted code to execute within the chain, which is how a software-only compromise reaches beneath the operating system.

The hardware root of trust that anchors this check is itself a physical assumption rather than an absolute: the boot ROM is typically immutable mask ROM, so where it is a flaw in it is permanent and unpatchable, and glitching it can bypass signature verification altogether [@ncc-mediatek-bootrom] -- a class not specific to one vendor [@checkm8-vu]; some systems-on-chip instead expose a ROM-patch path: a small vendor-signed patch, held in one-time-programmable fuses, that redirects the boot ROM's early execution and is itself locked by a fuse [@opentitan-rom-patch]. Immutability is therefore a vendor design choice rather than a constant, and either way the mechanism is the vendor's and not the owner's; and being signed code gated by fuses, that patch path is itself subject to the same key-compromise and glitching failure modes, and adds early-boot attack surface of its own. An invasive silicon attack can further read out the fused device keys beneath the chain [@ioactive-antifuse]; but every such defeat documented to date requires physical possession of the device and belongs to the forensic and unlock class, not to any remote, in-the-wild implant on a locked consumer phone. A remote-seeded, locally-triggered variant -- malware or a malicious update planting malformed data that an early boot stage parses on the next boot -- is conceivable in principle, but none has been documented, and on a locked device the code the boot ROM loads is signature-verified against a fuse-anchored key [@aosp-verified-boot], so a purely over-the-air boot-ROM compromise is not a realistic or observed attack surface.

The deeper point for the owner is epistemic: on the stock, vendor-keyed configuration the green boot state the device shows and the attestation certificate it presents are the platform's own self-report, which a sufficiently deep breach can forge and which the owner has no independent means to check. This limit is the hardware beneath the chain, not the verified-boot signing key above it: on devices that support it, Pixel among them, the owner can enroll a custom signing key and re-lock to it, reaching a yellow boot state whose on-screen key fingerprint the owner can themselves verify [@aosp-boot-flow], [@aosp-device-state] -- what stays beyond any owner is the boot ROM and the fused keys under that key, which none can replace or read out.

A fourth objection is that consumer antivirus and mobile-security apps -- products such as Kaspersky, Avast, and Norton, alongside Google's built-in Play Protect -- already let an owner detect compromise. These are real and widely used: Play Protect is, in Google's description, "the most widely deployed mobile threat protection service in the world" and "scans 200 billion Android apps daily" [@google-play-protect]. But all of them, however privileged, run inside the very system they are meant to vet. The third-party products operate as ordinary applications, while Play Protect is more privileged still -- a component of Google Mobile Services that reaches across the device to disable or remove other apps on the owner's behalf [@google-play-protect-remove], [@google-gms] -- yet it too executes within the running operating system rather than from any external, trusted vantage. They match applications and files against known-malware signatures and heuristics within the storage the operating system permits them to see -- Kaspersky's full scan, for instance, covers "the device's entire file system" [@kaspersky-android] -- which catches known, application-level threats but presupposes an operating system that is reporting truthfully about itself.

The vendors say as much. Avast states the limit plainly: "Like all applications on the Android market, it is subject to limitations imposed by the operating system version running on the device. It cannot protect against exploits targeting specific vulnerabilities in the OS kernel, the network stack and other fundamental parts of the system." [@avast-mobile-faq] That is exactly the class of compromise owner-inspectability concerns: a kernel-level or sub-operating-system implant sits beneath the vantage point these tools occupy and can neutralize or evade an in-system scanner whatever its privileges, as Sections 9 and 14 develop. Consumer antivirus therefore catches the known and the application-level but does not supply the external, trusted vantage point verification requires -- narrowing the gap for commodity malware without closing it for the targeted, low-level compromise, and so consistent with the analysis above.

## 13. Owner-Controlled Inspection and the Verified-Inspection Gap

The objections above return repeatedly to a single missing capability. Owner-inspectability is foreclosed because the privileged access an owner needs in order to inspect the system -- to examine it from a clean vantage, to detect a kernel-level rootkit or vendor-supplied code the owner cannot audit, and to let independent researchers work on hardware they own -- is exactly the access that forfeits the device's attested standing. The capability this bind calls for is what this paper calls a verified owner-inspection mode: owner-controlled privileged inspection that surrenders neither verified boot nor the attestation a relying party accepts. This section asks whether such a capability exists. It proposes and evaluates no system; it systematizes the approaches that have been shipped or proposed, locates each against the verified-boot security model, and identifies the gap none of them closes -- an open problem whose resolution would widen owner-inspectability well beyond the few who can tolerate the attestation penalty.

Several security-focused aftermarket systems decline to support root for this reason. CalyxOS "does not support running in rooted mode, nor do we recommend this," on the ground that "Running any Android device with root permissions severely undermines the security of the device" [@calyxos-root-faq]; the discontinued DivestOS likewise "does not support or encourage the use of root or runtime modification frameworks," which "break the trust model that AOSP employs and reduce the integrity of the system" [@divestos-root-faq].

That trust model is itself platform-centric: the Android platform security model requires agreement among user, platform, and developer, and it classes rooting, "both intentional and malicious," as "a non-compliant change which violates CDD" -- the Compatibility Definition Document a device must satisfy to ship as Android [@aosp-cdd] -- holding that ownership is "a legal concept rather than a technical one and therefore outside the scope of an OS security model" [@mayrhofer-android-security-model].

GrapheneOS has weighed the question directly, and its lead developer has addressed it in more technical detail: a carefully constrained root -- "an on-device root terminal which requires a special boot mode and isn't persistent," limited to the user and barred from hijacking by apps -- is "theoretically possible to do it in a way that doesn't throw away a lot of security," but "isn't being done by anyone right now"; the phrase "totally ruining verified boot by trusting persistent state" is directed instead at the persistent, fully-trusted way the root-enabled forks integrate it [@grapheneos-root-statement]. The project's own account put that cost plainly: once persistent state and the application layer are fully trusted, "the security model for verified boot and attestation are not intact to detect" a resulting compromise [@grapheneos-root-forum].

The boundedness of root established in Section 3 is thus not merely a restriction the stock vendor imposes and an aftermarket system could lift for free: a persistent root exacts a cost, and that cost is borne by the owner who enables it. An owner can relock under a self-signed key and keep cryptographic verified boot even with root present [@resign-android-image], and the vendor-anchored attestation verdict that stock relying parties demand is in any case already forfeited by leaving the vendor's key behind, rooted or not; but a persistent, fully-trusted superuser defeats the verified-boot security model of not trusting persistent state, so the self-signed state the owner could otherwise check no longer tells them the device is untampered. That owner-side verifiability is what a persistent root costs, and a root that keeps it is, by these projects' own accounts, unbuilt where it is thought possible at all and rejected outright elsewhere. Hardware attestation is designed around exactly this: GrapheneOS's own documentation notes that the attestation API reports the device's state "in a way that even an attacker exploiting the OS after boot to gain root cannot trivially bypass" [@grapheneos-attestation-compat].

Restricting attention to what an owner can do while keeping the bootloader locked and verified boot engaged, the approaches differ on four things (Table 4): whether it grants a full superuser, whether it persists across reboot, whether it modifies the system through a persistent overlay in unverified `/data`, and whether they preserve the verified-boot security model under which the owner can still detect tampering. A full superuser here is unrestricted uid-0 over the running system -- reading and writing any file, inspecting or altering any process, and reaching a raw block device or kernel memory, unbounded by the SELinux domains that confine exploit-gained root (Section 3) -- as distinct from a privilege capped below it (the ADB-shell permission tier) or confined to a signed, policy-bounded capability (a verified extension); it is in every case an in-system privilege short of the administrative control defined in Section 3, which none of these approaches confer, and the configurations that hold it are equal in privilege, differing only in how it is reached and whether it persists. The pattern is that the model is forfeited in two cases, both leaving unverified persistent state the owner cannot detect tampering in: a persistent, fully-trusted superuser that modifies the running system through a module store in `/data` re-applied at every boot, and a persistently writable `/system` reached by disabling its dm-verity hashtree in the owner-signed image. A configuration keeps the model when it breaks that chain at any link: by not granting a full superuser (a bounded verified extension, or shell-tier brokering), by not persisting (a non-persistent special-boot-mode root), or by keeping even a full, persistent superuser entirely inside the owner-signed, verity-covered image with no unverified persistent state (ADB-root baked into a re-signed image).

Table 4. Owner root and privilege options that keep the bootloader locked and verified boot engaged.

| Approach | Full superuser? | Persists across reboot? | Persistent `/system` overlay from `/data`? | Verified-boot security model preserved (owner can detect tampering)? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Root baked into a signed image and relocked under an owner key: a Magisk-style systemless patch (avbroot [@avbroot]) | Yes -- app-facing `su` | Yes | Yes -- module store in `/data/adb/modules` [@magisk-guides] | No -- verified boot no longer certifies the running system: the module store is magic-mounted onto `/system` at every boot [@magisk-details], so the running system depends on it, yet it lives in `/data`, the writable user-data partition [@aosp-file-based-encryption], outside the read-only verity hashtree that covers `/system` [@aosp-dm-verity], [@kernel-dm-verity] |
| Scoped kernel root baked in the same way: KernelSU, with per-app profiles [@kernelsu] | Yes -- in the kernel; per-app-profile gated (narrows the surface, not a security boundary) | Yes | Yes -- `/data/adb/modules`, overlaid on `/system` via OverlayFS [@kernelsu-modules] | No -- the same violation as the row above, with the superuser in the kernel rather than in `init`: the module store is overlaid onto `/system` at every boot [@kernelsu-modules], so the running system depends on it, yet it sits in `/data` [@aosp-file-based-encryption], outside the read-only verity hashtree covering `/system` [@aosp-dm-verity], [@kernel-dm-verity] |
| Persistent `/system` read-write: dm-verity for `/system` disabled in the owner-signed image (`vbmeta` hashtree-disabled flag, or `vbmeta` without `/system`'s hashtree descriptor), relocked under the owner's key (avbroot [@avbroot], [@aosp-avb-readme]) | Yes -- `/system` itself persistently writable (direct, not an overlay) | Yes | No -- direct writes to the `/system` partition, not an overlay | No -- the `vbmeta` still verifies under the owner's key, so the device boots locked, in the YELLOW state [@aosp-avb-readme]; but with dm-verity disabled `/system` falls outside the hashtree [@aosp-dm-verity], so tampering of `/system` is undetectable and the writable `/system` is unverified persistent state |
| Non-persistent root: a special-boot-mode terminal not retained across reboot [@grapheneos-root-statement] | Yes -- special-boot-mode terminal, no app-facing `su` | No | No | Yes -- the root does not persist across reboot, which restores the verified boot chain and system image (writable user data, outside verified boot, is a separate matter) |
| Signed, verified extension shipped inside the image: Android APEX [@aosp-apex] (the Linux analogue, under a verity-and-signature image policy, is systemd-sysext [@systemd-sysext]) | No -- bounded, policy-confined verified capability | Yes | No -- shipped as a signed, verity-checked image | Yes -- a signed, verity-checked image, so its contents are verified rather than arbitrary mutable state |
| ADB-root baked into an owner-signed, relocked image: `adbd` patched to run as root, with no on-device `su` (resign-android-image [@resign-android-image]) | Yes -- reachable only over ADB, no app-facing `su` | Yes | No | Yes -- the root path ships inside the owner-signed, verity-covered image, so tampering remains detectable and a reboot restores the verified state; it keeps no unverified persistent state that can modify the running system, unlike a `/data` module store, because `/system` stays mounted read-only under verity and even this full root cannot persistently write it in place (device-authorization state such as the `adbd` key store sits in `/data`, outside verified boot, as other user data does) |
| Privileged but not root: ADB-shell brokering (Shizuku) [@shizuku] | No -- capped at the ADB shell permission set, via an app-broker API | Yes | No | Yes -- stock image unmodified |

Three qualifications attach to Table 4. The non-persistent row can be realized by a RAM-backed overlay, but an overlay's upper layer lies outside the verified-boot hashtree [@aosp-dm-verity], [@kernel-dm-verity], so the content it serves is unverified whatever backs it; ephemerality bounds the window of unverified operation rather than keeping the running system verified. And the design space is not closed in principle: image-based operating systems have prompted proposals for an owner-facing "developer mode" that would reconcile locally built or signed code with an immutable, cryptographically validated system, but such a mode remains a design proposal rather than a shipping feature [@poettering-developer-mode]. Third, the avbroot and resign-android-image rows assume the verity-preserving case: dm-verity stays enabled under the owner's key, so cryptographic verified boot remains intact, but its security model is not. While verity is preserved `/system` stays mounted read-only and cannot be written in place -- a `touch` there fails with a read-only-filesystem error [@aosp-dynamic-partitions] and a read-write remount is refused on a production build [@aosp-set-verity-service] -- so modifying the running system there takes either a `/data` overlay re-applied at boot (the Magisk and KernelSU rows) or disabling verity outright, and the ADB-root row's full, persistent root, confined to this read-only image, can persist nothing to `/system`. The persistent `/system` read-write row is the verity-disabling alternative: taking the hashtree off `/system` -- the `vbmeta` hashtree-disabled flag, or a `vbmeta` without `/system`'s hashtree descriptor -- places `/system` outside the verified hashtree, so cryptographic verified boot still covers the boot chain but no longer `/system`. Disabling the hashtree only removes the integrity check, though: where `/system` ships as EROFS -- the Enhanced Read-Only File System [@kernel-erofs] that modern Android selects for read-only partitions at build time (`BOARD_SYSTEMIMAGE_FILE_SYSTEM_TYPE := erofs`) [@aosp-erofs] -- it is read-only by design and cannot be remounted read-write at all, so a persistently writable `/system` additionally requires rebuilding the owner-signed image with a writable filesystem (ext4 or f2fs) in place of EROFS [@aosp-erofs]. The bootloader stays locked, because it verifies the `vbmeta` signature, not the verity policy the signed `vbmeta` declares [@aosp-avb-readme] (the AVB reference behavior; a specific vendor's firmware may differ).

A concrete form illustrates the persistent-root row. An owner could bake a root-accessible SSH daemon into the owner-signed image and authorize it with the very key that signs that image -- the owner's verified-boot public key placed in the daemon's list of authorized keys, so that a single owner key governs both boot verification and administrative login while its private half stays off the device. Cryptographic verified boot is preserved and the key hierarchy is consistent, yet a persistent root service is exactly the fully-trusted persistent state the model is built to distrust -- a verdict that holds whether it is reached locally or over the network, since what the model declines to trust is the persistent, fully-privileged state itself, not the transport that reaches it. A key-only daemon is in itself a mature and widely accepted surface; what the network transport adds is narrower -- compromise no longer requires physical possession of the device, which leaves behind the on-device, physically-present case on which the safe reading depends. Gated instead to a non-persistent maintenance mode, it collapses into the non-persistent row.

Role separation sharpens the same conclusion. Confining administrative capability to a separate account or a deliberately entered maintenance session, while the owner's daily session runs unprivileged with no reachable escalation path, applies the principle of least privilege [@saltzer-schroeder] and separation of duty [@clark-wilson] and keeps the everyday attack surface off the privileged path. This arrangement is also richer than the single-axis non-persistent root of Table 4, where privilege is simply transient and lost on reboot: a role-separated design can offer, as a boot-time choice, either an administrative session or an ordinary unprivileged one, and can make each independently persistent -- changes retained across reboot -- or non-persistent -- changes discarded on reboot -- a matrix of role against persistence rather than a single transient mode. But the added options do not change the verified-persistence crux, and most of the matrix reduces to cases already analysed. Persistence of the unprivileged session is unremarkable, since its writes land in `/data`, the user-data partition already outside verified boot (a separate matter, as above). A non-persistent administrative session collapses onto the non-persistent row: its mutations are discarded on reboot, which restores the verified system. The one cell that is genuinely distinct is a persistent administrative session, and it fares no better than any other persistent root. The account boundary alone does not preserve the verified-boot model: a persistent, writable administrative role is offline persistent root, so the model survives only if that maintenance context is itself re-sealed on exit -- its integrity re-measured against the owner's own key. In the owner-signed configuration the owner does hold that key and can re-seal [@avbroot]; the constraint is operational: secure practice keeps the private half off the device (only the public key is enrolled), so re-sealing is an off-device re-sign and reflash from a trusted workstation rather than an in-situ maintenance step, and keeping the key on the device to make it in-situ would reintroduce the persistent trusted secret the locked chain exists to avoid. Such a re-seal restores the owner's own verified state, not the vendor-anchored attestation verdict, and no shipping system integrates it.

Sound role separation also asks more of the maintenance role than mere separateness: the role should itself be userspace-hardened and purpose-narrowed, discouraging activities better performed unprivileged -- launching a general-purpose web browser from within the privileged session, say -- so the window in which full privilege is exposed carries the least attack surface it can. These are properties of the userspace design, not the verified-boot model: like the per-app-profile narrowing of the kernel-root row, they lower the risk of the privileged role being hijacked without changing whether the owner can cryptographically detect tampering, so they leave the Table 4 classification untouched; the specific userspace, role-based design that would realize them is out of scope here.

A distinct configuration should be set aside rather than placed among these rows. Android is built in three variants selected by `TARGET_BUILD_VARIANT` -- the production `user` build, and the `userdebug` and `eng` developer builds [@aosp-build-variants] -- and the two developer variants lower assurance by construction. They set `ro.debuggable=1`, where the `user` build sets `ro.secure=1` and `ro.debuggable=0` [@aosp-sysprop-config]; that debuggable flag is the precondition under which `adbd` keeps uid-0, since `adb root` restarts the daemon unconfined only on a debuggable build [@aosp-adbd-main]. The developer variants also ship AOSP's own trivial `su`, callable only from the `root` and `shell` UIDs [@aosp-su], and permit the `adb disable-verity` and `adb remount` path that a `user` build compiles out and refuses [@aosp-set-verity-service]. SELinux is not thereby turned off: the developer builds merely add a `su` domain, itself compiled in only under a `userdebug_or_eng` guard and marked `permissive`, while the remaining domains -- the HALs and system services -- stay enforcing [@aosp-sepolicy-su-te]. The result is a lowered-assurance engineering image -- root over ADB, a factory `su`, verity defeatable -- not the locked, owner-keyed, AVB-verified production state the rest of this section analyzes, and typically signed with public test keys rather than production release keys. Root on a developer build is therefore not a route to owner-inspectable production security but a separate configuration that trades assurance away up front; it sits outside Table 4's framing, which presumes a production image with the bootloader locked and verified boot engaged, and must not be conflated with it.

Set against the owner's actual goals, these configurations are not equivalent, but for the goal this paper centers they converge. Owner-inspection -- reading the system logs, reading every file on disk, imaging a partition, or snapshotting memory -- is a read-only, one-time act that needs neither persistence, nor an app-facing superuser, nor any write to the system, so every full-root configuration in Table 4 satisfies it: the Magisk and KernelSU rows, the verity-disabled `/system` row, the non-persistent special-boot-mode root, and even the ADB-only root. The two that are not a full superuser do not -- the bounded verified extension reaches none of it, and the shell-tier broker reaches the log buffer but not another application's private storage, a raw block device, or kernel memory. Inspection is thus the easy half of the capability; what binds the owner is not reaching it but the attestation penalty that reaching it exacts. The other goals owners pursue -- debloating, system-wide ad-blocking, firewall control, and more -- divide across these configurations less uniformly, as Appendix B maps.

No approach in this design space, then, delivers all three of persistent owner administrative control, the verified-boot security model, and a relying-party-accepted attestation at once. The configurations that keep the security model do so by breaking the model-forfeiting chain at some link: not persisting (the non-persistent root), not granting a full superuser (a verified extension, or shell-tier brokering), or keeping even a full, persistent superuser entirely inside the owner-signed, verity-covered image with no unverified `/data` state (the ADB-root re-sign). What forfeits the model is unverified persistent state the owner cannot check, in either of two forms: a mutable `/data` module store overlaid on `/system`, or a persistently writable `/system` with its dm-verity hashtree disabled. Relying-party attestation is in any case given up by every one of these owner-keyed configurations, which no longer carry the vendor's key. A capability that closed the gap would have to assemble three things the shipped approaches reach only in pairs: privileged inspection the owner can invoke; an integrity state the owner, and ideally an independent verifier, can still check afterward -- which means privilege that either does not survive a reboot or arrives as a signed, verity-covered extension, and, where a change is meant to persist, a system re-signed under the owner's own key to restore the locked, verified state, a re-seal that (the owner's private key being kept off the device) is an off-device re-sign and reflash from a trusted workstation rather than an on-device step; and, for the relying-party half, an independent trust anchor for owner-enrolled keys that the attestation ecosystem does not presently provide. These three requirements are not alike in who can satisfy them. The first two are device-level and within the owner's reach: privilege that is discarded on reboot, or delivered as a signed, verity-covered extension -- re-signed where a change is kept, under the owner's own key -- already assembles privileged inspection with an integrity state the owner, and an independent verifier, can re-check, so the owner-side half is largely a matter of combining shipped mechanisms rather than inventing new ones. The third is not the owner's to satisfy. A relying-party-accepted verdict is anchored, by construction, to the vendor's root of trust, and the attestation ecosystem does not accept owner-enrolled keys (Appendix A); nor, on the available evidence, does it have reason to, since the same verified device state is a precondition for hardware-backed content protection [@patat-widevine], [@rafi-drm-mobile], [@aosp-drm] and for the platform-policy objectives the model is built to enforce [@mayrhofer-android-security-model], [@doctorow-general-computation]. The open problem is therefore asymmetric. Owner-side verifiability is attainable today for owners who accept the loss of vendor-gated attestation; re-admitting an owner-controlled device to the relying-party ecosystem is not a missing device mechanism but a policy choice that rests with the platform, and the content-protection and platform-control interests give it a standing reason to withhold. Treating the owner-side half as an open engineering problem, rather than a settled impossibility, is what would let owner-inspectability -- and with it independent malware and backdoor investigation -- reach beyond the owners who can accept the attestation penalty.

## 14. Discussion

The limitations examined here are not independent defects but facets of a single design choice: administrative capability is withheld from the device owner and conferred only on the vendor and manufacturer. Verified boot, sandboxing, mandatory access control, attestation, and hardware-bound key storage each advance the platform's assurance that only approved, unmodified software runs, and each simultaneously removes a means by which an owner might inspect the device from a trusted vantage point. The circular trust dependency of Section 8 is the sharpest expression of the tension: the configurations an owner would need in order to verify the device are exactly those the platform refuses to attest as trusted.

The owner is consequently placed in a position analogous to that of an attacker with respect to introspection, and in one respect a more constrained one: these controls foreclose the owner's sole avenue -- modifying the device to inspect it -- while leaving open the runtime and sub-operating-system routes the capable adversary of Sections 8 and 12 uses, and the owner brings none of an attacker's willingness to damage the device. The practical recourses -- JTAG-based memory acquisition, chip-level storage extraction, and exploit-driven temporary root -- are, respectively, rarely available, destructive or specialized, and dependent on the existence of exploitable vulnerabilities that the platform is otherwise trying to eliminate. The physical-acquisition path is further foreclosed where unlocking the bootloader triggers a mandatory wipe of user data, so that gaining access to the storage also erases its contents [@aosp-bootloader-locking]. None of these recourses offers the routine, non-destructive, owner-driven verification that the threat model of Section 3 calls for, and, as Sections 3 and 8 established, even a successful temporary root yields only a bounded in-system privilege on a device that has already forfeited its attested standing -- not the external, trusted vantage point that verification requires. Nor would more privilege close the gap: a kernel-space rootkit manipulates the very kernel structures that any in-system observer, root included, must query, so it can neutralize a privileged security tool and falsify its results regardless of the privilege level at which that tool runs [@guri-joker-jtag], [@bickford-rootkits-smartphones]. Subversion-resistant detection must therefore observe the system from outside the running operating system [@guri-joker-jtag], [@garfinkel-vmi], echoing the long-established principle that a system cannot be trusted to report truthfully on its own state [@thompson-trusting-trust], [@rutkowska-state-harmful].

The contrast with a fully controlled host is one of category, not merely degree. On a desktop or server the baseline acquisition workflow -- detach the storage and image it through a write blocker, a device that passes read commands while blocking any that would alter the drive, so the acquisition does not modify the evidence [@nist-sp800-101r1], [@nist-hwb-spec], then decrypt the image with the owner's passphrase-derived key -- is available to an owner who can remove the storage and holds the decryption key, and remains so even though the storage device itself runs opaque controller firmware that a determined attacker can subvert: compromising the firmware of a commercial off-the-shelf drive to backdoor data as it is written has been demonstrated using only public information and reverse engineering, installable by ordinary malware once the operating system is compromised [@zaddach-hdd-backdoor], and such drive-firmware implants have been found in the wild [@kaspersky-equation], with persistence that survives disk formatting and a full operating-system reinstall [@kaspersky-equation-qa]. Implants one layer lower still, in the motherboard's own UEFI flash, persist even across a storage replacement, because they do not reside on the storage at all [@eset-lojax]. That residual is real, but it is a different thing from the phone's barrier: an advanced and partially mitigable attack vector layered beneath an acquisition path that otherwise works, not a categorical barrier for all owners.

On a stock phone the same baseline workflow is foreclosed at its first step, because the storage is soldered, and at its last, because even a successful chip-level read returns the user's data as ciphertext under a hardware-bound key. The firmware-implant concern, common to both platforms and marking the floor of what either owner can verify, thus compounds on the phone a barrier that is already categorical rather than standing, as on the host, as the one residual beneath an otherwise working path.

Hardware memory acquisition sharpens the same contrast, but only partly, because it is closing on both sides. On a conventional computer an examiner can in principle capture RAM from outside the running operating system -- by direct memory access over an expansion bus [@markettos-thunderclap], [@frisk-pcileech] or by exploiting the residual charge in DRAM after a cold reboot [@halderman-coldboot] -- yet modern platforms increasingly block such access with an input-output memory management unit [@markettos-thunderclap], and cold-boot capture needs physical access to the memory. A stock phone forecloses even this residual avenue: its memory is soldered in package-on-package stacks that cannot be transplanted, it exposes no external bus capable of such access, and the one demonstrated on-device cold-boot variant first required unlocking the bootloader, which wipes the user data it sought to recover [@muller-frost-coldboot].

Table 5 sets the owner's control over each layer of the stack side by side for the two platforms, read downward from the bootloader to the firmware beneath the operating system. The pattern is a categorical difference across the upper, operating-system-and-boot layers, where the personal-computer owner holds administrative control and the stock-phone owner holds none, and a shared opaque floor at the firmware and management-coprocessor layers below them, where neither owner can inspect what runs.

Table 5. Owner control over the stack: a traditional modular personal computer the owner administers versus stock Android (as sold).

| Stack layer | Traditional personal computer (owner) | Stock Android, as sold (owner) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bootloader | Replaceable; Secure Boot is optional, and the owner can enroll custom keys or disable it | Locked and vendor-signed; unlocking wipes user data and drops attestation, and some devices are unlockable only by exploit or not at all |
| Kernel | Owner builds and boots an arbitrary kernel | Vendor-signed boot image; a custom kernel requires unlocking |
| Kernel modules | Owner loads arbitrary modules | Lockdown, module-signature, and dm-verity enforcement restrict loading |
| initramfs | Owner controls | Part of the signed boot image; not owner-modifiable on stock |
| `init` (first userspace, PID 1) | Owner chooses or replaces the init system, and can boot to an alternate `init` or single-user mode | Android `init` ships in the signed ramdisk and system partitions under verified boot and dm-verity, runs under SELinux, and is not owner-modifiable on stock |
| Operating system | Owner installs any operating system | A custom operating system requires unlocking and incurs an attestation penalty |
| In-system superuser (root) | Available to the owner by default | Withheld as sold; reachable only by exploit or unlocking -- exploit-gained root is SELinux-confined, while systemless root sidesteps SELinux but remains below the kernel's lockdown and module-signature enforcement |
| Boot an external trusted system | Yes, from external media | Not supported while locked: the bootloader boots only signed images and an external system requires first unlocking [@aosp-fastboot-protocol], [@aosp-bootloader-locking]; the path is then device-specific |
| Remove and image storage | Yes: detach the storage and image it on a trusted controller | Storage is soldered; chip-level reading is destructive, and the data returns as ciphertext under a hardware-bound key |
| Platform firmware | BIOS/UEFI: reflashable but vendor-signed and opaque in practice, and in-the-wild implants have been demonstrated [@eset-lojax] | Bootloader and vendor firmware: vendor-controlled and owner-opaque |
| Management co-processor | Intel ME or AMD PSP: a vendor-firmware co-processor the owner cannot inspect -- the ME embedded in the chipset with privileged access to platform data [@ermolov-intel-me], the AMD-SP a boot-time root of trust that runs before the main cores [@jacob-faultpm] | Cellular baseband, and any discrete secure element: vendor firmware on separate silicon the owner cannot inspect [@weinmann-baseband] |
| Storage-controller firmware | Opaque; controller-firmware implants demonstrated in the lab [@zaddach-hdd-backdoor] and found in the wild [@kaspersky-equation] | Opaque |

The division is systematic. On the personal computer the owner commands the entire operating-system-and-boot stack and is blocked only at the firmware and management-coprocessor floor; on the stock phone the owner commands essentially none of the stack, so the same firmware floor sits beneath a barrier that is already categorical at every layer above it. Administrative control, in the sense defined in Section 3, is exactly what the left column has and the right column lacks; root, where a phone owner reaches it, adds only the single "in-system superuser" row and leaves every other row unchanged.

The ease of this recourse may be overstated. Rooting presumes an act that retail devices are configured to resist: they ship with the bootloader locked, and unlocking it -- where the vendor permits it at all -- is gated behind an explicit warning and a mandatory wipe of user data [@aosp-bootloader-locking], [@aosp-boot-flow]. Many carrier- and vendor-locked devices cannot be unlocked by their owners at all, or only by means of an exploit [@bootloader-wall-of-shame]. Unlocking is widely said to void the device warranty, although consumer-protection law in the United States and the European Union limits blanket voiding and requires a defect to be causally linked to the modification before coverage may be refused [@magnuson-moss-act], [@eu-directive-2019-771]. Several mainstream vendors attach irreversible hardware-level consequences to unlocking, by distinct mechanisms rather than a single universal one: one permanently trips a one-time-programmable e-fuse that disables hardware-backed attestation and the protected features that depend on it and cannot be reset [@samsung-knox-hw-security]; another irrevocably erases device-unique keys that relocking does not restore [@sony-unlock-bootloader]; and one-time-programmable (eFuse) hardware is, more generally, the standard system-on-chip primitive for secure-boot enablement and anti-rollback enforcement, whose blowing is a one-way operation [@qualcomm-secure-boot].

A countervailing point is that not every platform is irreversible: on some devices unlocking can be reversed and the verified-boot and attestation state is restored on relocking -- to the full state under the vendor's stock key, or to a reduced state under an owner-enrolled key [@aosp-verified-boot], [@aosp-key-id-attestation]. Even there the bind holds, because the reversible path returns the owner to the vendor-keyed configuration without ever granting the top attestation standing while the device remains open to inspection.

Taken together, these frictions make unlocking a high-barrier step rather than a routine one. The privileged state an owner does reach is, as Section 8 established, counterproductive for verification; and the integrity-masking tools owners deploy to pass integrity checks work by presenting the profile of an unmodified stock device, leaving them in an arms race against detection in which the spoofing side has been progressively constrained [@nguyen-vu-android-rooting].

The unlock procedure is, moreover, not silent to software already resident on the device. Preparing a device for inspection changes observable system state, and unlocking the bootloader triggers a mandatory wipe that destroys the user-data partition before any image of it can be taken -- so the inspection a high-threat owner attempts can itself alert, and then erase, a resident implant.

That such environmental signals drive evasion is established rather than speculative: measurement studies show modern malware checks for virtualized or instrumented environments and withholds its behavior when it detects them [@chen-anti-virtualization], [@ruggia-evasive-malware], and the anti-forensics literature names the matching capabilities of artifact wiping, trail obfuscation, and self-removal [@garfinkel-anti-forensics], [@mitre-attack-evasion]. Mobile spyware has combined the two in the wild: NSO's Pegasus for Android shipped "suicide functionality to remove itself" [@lookout-chrysaor] (Suicide Functionality section), triggered among other cases when it cannot find a valid cellular subscriber identity -- behavior attributed to avoiding analysis and emulator environments -- while the iOS variant self-destructs when "a great probability of exposing the agent exists" [@marczak-million-dollar-dissident] (Section 3.5), and later versions were observed deleting their own process records to hide their traces [@amnesty-pegasus-methodology].

The specific loop -- an implant watching for unlock preparation and erasing itself before acquisition -- remains a reasoned extrapolation rather than a documented exploit; its constituent capabilities are each demonstrated, and the principle that a compromised system cannot be trusted to report truthfully on itself is long established [@thompson-trusting-trust], [@garfinkel-vmi]. The nearest realized case, the Rombertik malware, destroys its host on detecting that its own code has been analyzed [@talos-rombertik]; this is a genuine detect-then-destroy reaction, but it is keyed to tampering with the malware's own image rather than to a forensic acquisition, and it wrecks the host rather than quietly erasing the implant so the device passes inspection clean. No reported case closes the precise loop posited here.

### Limitations of this analysis

Several limitations bound the claims made here. The paper is a systematization of existing evidence, not a new empirical study: it reports no measurements of its own, and where the literature is thin -- notably on the precise prevalence of attestation-gated applications and on the fraction of devices for which hardware acquisition is feasible -- it says so rather than substituting an estimate. Its central construct, owner-inspectability, is a qualitative criterion rather than a metric; this paper defines and applies it but does not quantify it, and an operational scale by which devices and configurations could be scored is left to future work. The analysis is deliberately scoped to the owner's standpoint and to the Android platform as shipped on mainstream devices; it does not weigh the owner-side costs against the genuine ecosystem-wide benefits that the same mechanisms deliver against running adversaries, which the platform literature documents [@mayrhofer-android-security-model], and it does not extend its specific claims to other platforms except where a cited source does so. Finally, the platform is a moving target: verdict tiers, acquisition techniques, and project capabilities change between releases, and the specific mechanisms cited here are current as of writing rather than permanent.

Inspectability and modifiability are distinct as ends -- the criterion asks that the owner establish and demonstrate what the device runs, not that the device be freely modifiable -- yet on locked stock devices they remain entangled in practice, in three ways. Mechanically, gaining a first inspection foothold on such a device generally requires the same unlock that also admits an arbitrary replacement system, so the capability the owner needs tends to arrive bundled with one the platform withholds. Through the software-freedom ecosystem, the freedoms to study, rebuild, and redistribute sustain inspection from upstream: auditable source and reproducible builds give the owner a known-good reference to check an acquired image against -- they fix what a trusted build should contain, though confirming the device actually runs it still takes an acquired image or a trusted measurement -- and shared tools and findings are what make inspection practical at all. And since inspectability is only a proxy for the owner's actual end -- security for the owner, not the platform -- modification can reach that end by another route: replacing an opaque firmware component with a more trustworthy build can improve security where inspecting it is impossible, though it substitutes a better-placed trust rather than delivering the verification the criterion measures, the same move as re-locking to a different vendor's key (Section 12). That reaching inspection on a locked stock device usually routes through the broader modification the platform withholds is itself the case for decoupling the two by design.

A measured reading is that the platform optimizes for resistance to the ordinary running adversary at the cost of owner-side auditability, and that the two objectives, while not strictly incompatible, are in current designs rarely satisfied together. The forensic literature surveyed here does not claim that compromise is undetectable on stock devices; it claims that detection is constrained by observability, and that a negative result is accordingly weak evidence of integrity. Building on that, and on the user-controlled root of trust that AOSP allows in principle (Section 3) and the owner-signed GrapheneOS configuration of Section 12, this paper's own position is that auditability could be improved without abandoning existing protections. That position suggests a design space -- owner-attestable inspection modes, exportable integrity measurements, or supported trusted-boot verification paths -- in which verifiability and platform integrity might be advanced together rather than traded off.

## 15. Practical Consequences

The owner-inspectability gap is not an abstraction: it has a concrete, decade-old instance in Pegasus, the spyware developed and sold by the commercial surveillance vendor NSO Group. Pegasus has been documented exploiting users' devices in the open since its public disclosure in August 2016 [@marczak-million-dollar-dissident], [@lookout-pegasus-technical-analysis], with code artifacts indicating in-the-wild activity from as early as 2013 to 2014 [@lookout-pegasus-technical-analysis] and attacks recorded through 2021 [@amnesty-pegasus-methodology] -- roughly a decade in which one tool has remained effective against current devices. It is the work of an advanced adversary: Project Zero assessed one of its exploit chains as "one of the most technically sophisticated exploits we've ever seen" and judged that "the capabilities NSO provides rival those previously thought to be accessible to only a handful of nation states" [@projectzero-forcedentry]; Lookout called it "the most sophisticated privately-developed attack [it had] encountered on a mobile endpoint" [@lookout-pegasus-technical-analysis]; and it is sold only to governments [@marczak-million-dollar-dissident], with recent variants delivered zero-click [@amnesty-pegasus-methodology], [@projectzero-forcedentry].

This threat falls squarely in the class the owner cannot verify. The standard forensic response -- examine the device from a clean, trusted vantage point and work from a verified image -- is exactly what a stock locked device withholds (Sections 7, 9, 10), and the recent implant is engineered to leave nothing recoverable by the means available to the owner: it is non-persistent and memory-resident, so "binary payloads associated with these processes are not recoverable from the non-volatile filesystem"; "instead, one would need to be able to jailbreak the device without reboot, and attempt to extract payloads from memory" [@amnesty-pegasus-methodology]. That one path is the capability the locked device denies its owner (Section 13), and it is self-defeating, since the reboot clears the implant [@amnesty-pegasus-methodology]. And as Section 14 sets out, even a live, instrumented view can be turned against the examiner: Pegasus is documented to detect analysis and emulator conditions and to carry self-destruct functionality, so an implant can remove itself before it is seen [@lookout-chrysaor], [@marczak-million-dollar-dissident].

The consequence is a loop that closes against the owner, and it is not hypothetical. For the commodity, application-level threats most users face, on-device defenses work (Sections 6, 12); but for the targeted, state-grade class -- of which Pegasus is a real, long-running, widely deployed example -- the platform leaves the owner unable to establish either the presence or the absence of a compromise. That is the practical cost of withholding owner-inspectability: not that the device is defenseless, but that against the adversary who most warrants independent verification, the owner is left without the means to perform it.

## 16. Conclusion

Vendor-locked devices systematically limit the owner's ability to inspect, control, and independently verify the software they run. Stock Android, among the most openly specified such platforms and this paper's worked example, is the case developed here: the owner cannot practically examine the whole system from a clean, trusted environment; application and update delivery is mediated by centralized providers whose abuse could enable targeted malicious installation; internal storage and live memory are inaccessible without withheld privileges; attestation couples a device's attested standing to the device remaining unmodified; low-level rootkit detection generally demands a trusted memory snapshot taken from outside the running system; and the established techniques of host-based malware analysis are, as a class, unavailable without first breaking the verified-boot and attestation guarantees the platform enforces.

Obtaining root narrows these limits without lifting them. It lets an owner inspect at-rest storage, installed packages, and process memory -- enough to find commodity, application-level, and at-rest malware -- but not the targeted low-level class: root remains an in-system vantage point that a kernel-space rootkit can deceive, it does not reach the kernel, boot chain, or firmware beneath it, and -- whether gained by exploit or by unlocking -- it forfeits the attested status the owner is trying to preserve. Backing up the hardware-bound encryption key is likewise impractical for most owners.

The existence of consensual forensic tooling, and of aftermarket operating systems such as GrapheneOS, does not overturn these conclusions, and it does not locate the gap in those systems. Such tooling is bounded by the limited diagnostic data the platform exposes, and owner-inspectable configurations are reachable only by leaving the as-sold device behind and accepting the attestation penalty the platform's ecosystem imposes on any configuration not signed by the vendor's key -- the loss of device-integrity standing that leads an apparently growing set of applications to refuse to run -- so an absence of findings on a stock device remains weak evidence of integrity. The aftermarket systems in fact afford the owner more administrative control, not less, and so demonstrate that owner-inspectability is a feasible configuration rather than a hardware necessity: what withholds it from the as-sold device is the vendor's design decision to confer administrative capability on the vendor and manufacturer alone, and what withholds a relying-party-accepted standing from an owner-controlled configuration is a platform policy choice rather than a missing device mechanism (Sections 13, 14). These limits are therefore not accidents of one vendor's engineering but consequences of vendor-lock itself, and the owner's structural position recurs, to varying degrees, on other vendor-locked devices. Treating security as owner-side verifiability, rather than as platform-side policy enforcement, makes these limitations visible as a coherent whole and identifies owner-inspectability as a property in need of improvement.

Verification looks the attested key up in the table for the reported boot state and rejects any key that is absent, so a build signed with a key not compiled in -- including an owner's self-signed build of otherwise unmodified GrapheneOS -- is refused rather than attested:

```
final RootOfTrust.VerifiedBootState verifiedBootState = rootOfTrust.verifiedBootState;
final String verifiedBootKey = BaseEncoding.base16().encode(rootOfTrust.verifiedBootKey);
final DeviceInfo device;
if (verifiedBootState == RootOfTrust.VerifiedBootState.SELF_SIGNED) {
    if (attestationSecurityLevelEnum == ParsedAttestationRecord.SecurityLevel.STRONG_BOX) {
        device = fingerprintsStrongBoxNonStock.get(verifiedBootKey);
    } else {
        device = fingerprintsNonStock.get(verifiedBootKey);
    }
} else if (verifiedBootState == RootOfTrust.VerifiedBootState.VERIFIED) {
    if (attestationSecurityLevelEnum == ParsedAttestationRecord.SecurityLevel.STRONG_BOX) {
        device = fingerprintsStrongBoxStock.get(verifiedBootKey);
    } else {
        device = fingerprintsStock.get(verifiedBootKey);
    }
} else {
    throw new GeneralSecurityException("verified boot state is not verified or self signed");
}

if (device == null) {
    throw new GeneralSecurityException("invalid verified boot key fingerprint: " + verifiedBootKey);
}
```

It states only that an alternative operating system's verified-boot key must be "included in the Auditor app and Attestation Server." Recognizing an owner-signed build therefore means editing these hardcoded tables in the source and rebuilding the Auditor app: local second-device verification needs only that [@grapheneos-auditor-tutorial], while scheduled remote verification [@grapheneos-auditor-tutorial] additionally requires rebuilding the app to point at a different server -- the released Auditor pins attestation.app and rejects any other [@grapheneos-auditor-domain], [@grapheneos-auditor-issue140] -- and self-hosting a correspondingly keyed attestation server [@grapheneos-attestation-server].

## Appendix B. Rooting use cases and the capability each requires

Owners root for goals beyond the inspection this paper centers on, and a reader weighing the configurations of Table 4 will want to know whether a given one meets their own. The motivations documented in the rooting literature recur across sources: removing preinstalled applications, running applications that require root, and bypassing carrier and manufacturer restrictions [@zhang-root-providers], [@sun-android-rooting], [@nguyen-vu-android-rooting]. To our knowledge, no peer-reviewed study quantifies their relative prevalence with a sound methodology; the taxonomies agree on the categories rather than their shares. This appendix maps the common goals to the minimum capability each requires -- in the terms of Section 3 and Table 4 -- and to the configurations that supply it. Owner-inspection (reading logs, reading all files, imaging storage, snapshotting memory, and full device backup) is treated in the body (Sections 7, 9, 10, 11) and, as Section 13 notes, is met by every full-root configuration; the remaining goals map less uniformly.

Two qualifications sharpen the debloating rows. The removal owners most often perform is a per-user disable or uninstall -- the platform's own `pm uninstall --user 0` and `pm disable-user`, an operation at the ADB-shell permission tier that the shell user is allowed to run without root [@aosp-shell-manifest]: it records a per-user uninstalled or disabled mark in `/data` and leaves the `/system` APK in place [@aosp-packagemanager-flags], so it needs no system write (the app is removed only for that user, reclaims no `/system` space, and returns for a new user or after a factory reset), and it is automated by tools built on it [@universal-android-debloater]. Only true removal from the image -- reclaiming the space, or removing the app for every user -- requires a persistent system write, and so one of the three write-capable configurations. Those three in turn split on injection: the verity-disabled `/system` configuration can place or replace files, but the pinning-bypass and framework-hooking goals additionally need an app-facing superuser and Zygote injection [@aosp-zygote], [@magisk-guides], which only the Magisk and KernelSU rows provide. The pinning-bypass goal is the narrower exception: for a repackageable application an owner can strip its pinning by patching and reinstalling the app at the ADB-shell tier, without root [@owasp-mastg-repackaging], so superuser and Zygote injection are the minimum only for in-place, system-wide injection, and repackaging fails against an application that verifies its own signature or enforces a server-backed integrity check.

Two further points sharpen the remaining rows. The last row's "module store" is the systemless userspace module store at `/data/adb/modules` [@magisk-guides], [@kernelsu-modules] -- the integrity-masking modules of Section 8, overlaid on `/system` at runtime -- not Linux kernel module loading. What such modules pass is the software tier only: app-side root detection and the basic verdict, a signal a locked or unlocked device can satisfy [@play-integrity-verdicts], and, on devices launched before Android 13, the legacy device verdict [@playintegrityfork]; they do not pass the hardware-backed device or strong verdict, which demands a certified, vendor-keyed, locked image (Section 8, Appendix A) [@play-integrity-verdicts]. Installing either tool first requires unlocking the bootloader to flash the patched image [@magisk-install]; the owner may then re-lock to their own enrolled key -- the locked, owner-keyed yellow state Table 4 assumes [@aosp-boot-flow], not the stock vendor-keyed one -- which still forfeits the hardware-backed verdict [@resign-android-image]. Finally, the ad-blocking and custom-CA goals are not met by a bounded verified extension: an APEX activates only its own signed namespace and does not overlay arbitrary `/system` paths [@aosp-apex], and the custom-CA store, on Android 14 and later, is the vendor-signed Conscrypt APEX (`/apex/com.android.conscrypt/cacerts`) that an owner cannot inject into, with the legacy `/system/etc/security/cacerts` path applying on earlier releases [@aosp-conscrypt] -- so these goals need a persistent `/system` write (or, on recent Android, an overlay of the APEX store), which keeps the verified-boot model only when it is an owner-signed image rebuild rather than a `/data` overlay or a verity-disabled `/system`.
